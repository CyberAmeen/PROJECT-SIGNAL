2. Description of the Action: The Corps of Engineers proposes to:
(1) Construct an 800-foot breakwater extension north from the end of the
present breakwater paralleling channel alinement; (2) raiss the top ele­
vation of the existing breakwater; and (3) if later determined necessary
to control channel shoaling, construct a 400-foot long groin on the east
side of the Charleston Channel.

3.    Summary of Environmental Effects:

The proposed extension is expected to cause the sand spit to reestablish
where it existed adjacent to the breakwater until 1977. The shape of
the spit and the added height of the existing breakwater are expected to
reduce wave activity in the boat basin, reduce damage to boats and
structures caused by surging, and help to maintain channel depth and
alinement.

During construction of the proposed project, trucking and placing of stone
would produce a temporary increase in turbidity, noise, dust, and traffic
congestion of the construction site. Toe trench excavation would occur at
the same time the extension would be constructed causing localized turbid­
ity throughout the construction period. The approximately 10,000 cubic
yards of sand removed would be placed on and adjacent to the toe of the
extension resulting in some burial of organisms, some siltation of filter­
ing animals and some minor food web disruption. Organisms present in the
removed sand would be distrupted or killed. Fish in the area would also
be disrupted as the stone is placed. The breakwater extension and reha­
bilitation would cover about three acres of sandy subtidal habitat and
create 0.4 acres of intertidal rocky habitat. Benthic organisms (includ­
ing razor clams, cockle clams, bull kelp, snails, and various worms and
larvae) would be covered and their habitat permanently altered.
The newly created rocky habitat would encourage establishment of species
similar to those inhabitating the jetty area, such as rockfish, sculpins,
surfperch, greenling, anenomes, mussels, barnacles, and attached alg.'e.

The sand spit which would form west of the extension would contain about
11 acres of sand above MHHW and cause 12 acres of intertidal sandy habi­
tat to accrete. Present razor clam populations would survive the gradual
sand build up and new larval clams should settle on the beach to maintain
the present population.

Some shoaling would occur both north and south of the proposed groin structure
which would cover one acre of rocky and sandy, subtidal benthic habitat.

Dredging requirements in Charleston Channel will be reduced after construc­
tion of the extension; fewer new disposal sites would be required.

Socio-economic effects of the project center around benefits which would
occur to the fish industry. Monetary losses and time delays currently
experienced by local fishermen should decrease substantially. The expected
reduction in surge problems may encourage local authorities to push for
expansion of the boat basin.

4. Alternatives Considered? Structural solutions analyzed include con­
struction of the following: an L-shaped detached breakwater to the north
of the present breakwater, plus 3 groins on the east side of the Charleston
Channel; a 1,000-foot northward extension of the existing breakwater; an
800-foot northward extension of the existing breakwater and one groin on
the east side of Charleston Channel; a single groin on the east side of
Charleston Channel; and an 800-foot northward extension of the existing
breakwater, plus 2 groins on the east side of the Charleston Channel.
Other alternatives include no action and dredging Charleston Channel to
a new alinement.

1.2   Relationship of this EIS to the Coos Bay Navigation Project

Federal involvement in the existing Coos Bay navigation project began with
the River and Harbor Act of 1910 and was extended in Acts of 1919, 1922,
1926, 1930, 1935, 1946, 1948, and 1970. Under the authority of these
Acts, the Corps maintains:

         Two jetties protecting the bay entrance;

         a navigation channel 40 feet deep over the entrance bar, decreas­
         ing to a 36-foot depth from Guano Rock (mile 1) to Isthmus Slough
         (mile 15);

         two turning basins (opposite Coalbank Slough and at North Bend);

         an anchorage basin; and

         a small boat basin at Charleston.

In the River and Harbor Act of 1970, Congress authorized the following
project modifications, which are currently being completed (fig. 1-1):

         A 45-foot deep, 700-foot wide channel across the entrance bar,
         decreasing gradually to 35 feet of depth and 300 feet of width
         near Guano Rock;

         an inner channel 35 feet deep and 300 feet wide from Guano Rock
         to about river mile 9, then widening to 400 feet (same depth) to
         river mile 15;.

         enlarged turning basins with depths of 35 feet at North Bend (800
         feet by 1,000 feet) and Coalbank Slough (700 feet by 1,000 feet);

         an anchorage area 800 feet wide and 1,000 feet long at mile 5.5;
         and

         abandonment of previously authorized anchorage at river mile 3.5
         and 7.0.

The Charleston project is proposed under the authority provided in the
River and Harbor Act of 1948.

1.3   Relationships to Other Federal Actions

The U. S. Coast Guard Station at Coos Head provides navigational aid for
the Coos Bay Channel.

South Slough, an estuarine sanctuary jointly funded by the Office of
Coastal Zone Management, U. S. Department of Commerce and by the State of
Oregon, is located just south of Charleston. Creation of the sanctuary
was described in a 1974 Final EIS prepared by the Department of Commerce.
Additional activities in the Coos Bay area may develop in the future. The
Port of Coos Bay has applied for two permits, one for enlargement of the
Charleston Boat Basin (#071 OYA2-002922) and one for development of a
trawler basin on the North Spit (#071 OYA2-002884).


The Corps also maintains harbors at several other ports along the coast.
The nearest deep draft harbors are located at Newport, Oregon and Eureka,
California.

1.4   Existing Conditions

In 1956, a 10-foot deep channel was dredged to connect Charleston with
the main Coos Bay Channel. A small boat basin with spaces for 244 boats
was also constructed. The boat basin was expanded to 544 spaces in 1966.
The Charleston Channel has been maintained by frequent dredging since 1961.

Maintaining proper channel depth and alinement has become increasingly
difficult. Waves entering the main channel transport sand from the ocean
and from the shoal area to the north of the boat basin's breakwater; the
sand is deposited in the Charleston Channel, tending to form an eastward
bend in the channel (fig. 1-3). Many boats have sustained hull damage
at this location, and Coast Guard ships are often forced to remain
outside the channel entrance during periods of low water and high waves.


High waves entering Coos Bay during winter storms occasionally overtop the
present Charleston breakwater, which was built to an elevation of 14 feet
above mean lower low water (+14' MLLW). The breakwater, consequently,
has been damaged and part of the blacktop road and a water supply line on
top of the breakwater have been destroyed.

During fall and winter of 1977 and 1978, the sand spit usually located
next to the northern edge of the breakwater completely disappeared. Due
to this spit erosion, more wave energy was able to reach the boat basin;
damage has correspondingly increased. Waves entering the main channel
are diffracted around the end of the breakwater as well as reflect from
the shoreline at Barview.  Surge damages in the boat basin result.

To illustrate the severity of the problem, a single storm in October, 1977
caused more than $30,000 damage to mooring structures in the basin. Other
storms during the 1977-78 winter season inflicted much more damage to
boats, mooring lines, pilings, and docks (fig. 1-4).

1.5   Proposed Action

The proposed action     (fig. 1-2) is to:

         Extend the present breakwater 800 feet to the north, parallel to
         the Charleston Channel;
          raise the elevation of the existing breakwater;

          possibly, construct a 400-foot long groin on the east side of the
          Charleston Channel to control shoaling.

The breakwater extension would have a top elevation of +14 feet MLLW and
a width, at its crest, of 16 feet. Base width would be 95 feet. About
40,700 tons of rock would be used to construct the extension; about 1.8
acres of present bay-bottom would be covered. Bottom elevation at this
location constantly changes; the latest available 1978 survey showed a
range of elevation from 0 to -1 MLLW. Outer stones in the breakwater
would have an average diameter of about 3.5 feet. Construction would be
accomplished by placing stone from the end of the present breakwater.
To stabilize the toe of the extension, a trench would be excavated along
both sides of the 800-foot extension (shown on Figure 1-2). Excavation of
this toe trench would involve the removal of approximately 10,000 cubic
yards of sand from the 8-foot wide by 8-foot deep trench. The trench
would be filled with large quarry stone and the sand, as it was removed,
would be placed on the toe and the exposed slope of the extension as it
is being constructed. A new spit of sand is expected to form along the
north side of the existing breakwater and along the west side of the
extension; it should be about 11 acres in size above mean higher high
water.

The crest elevation of the present breakwater, currently variable, would
be raised to +18 feet MLLW for its entire length (about 2,000 feet).
About 0.8 acre of sandy intertidal and subtidal would be covered as a
result of raising the breakwater height. Rocks which presently form the
outer layer of the breakwater are about 2 to 3 tons each. Larger rocks
weighing about 3.5 tons each would be used to raise the breakwater to
the +18-foot MLLW elevation. About 25,200 tons of rock would be required.

The groin would be 400 feet long and would cover slightly over an acre of
subtidal bottom. The top elevation would be +14 feet MLLW and crest
width would be 12 feet. Rock sizes for groin construction would be
comparable to those for the breakwater extension and raising. About
35,600 tons of rock would be used for the groin; marine equipment would
be used.

The proposed action includes no new channel dredging. Maintenance dredging
of the Charleston Channel is discussed in the 0&M Final EIS (August, 1976),
available from Portland District at the address noted on the summary page.

Benefits accruing to improvements are based upon a 50-year economic life,
1978 price levels, and an interest rate of 6-7/8 percent. Benefits are

of basin and structure damages due to prevention of a breach in the
breakwater.

breakwater would eliminate the present annual repair costs. The break­

effort has been, to a large extent, replacement in kind. Maintenance
cost of the rehabilitation work now averages $22,000 per year.

frequently. The existing breakwater has recently suffered partial

is such that routine major rehabilitation is due. Should a major breach

2.    ENVIRONMENTAL SETTING WITHOUT THE PROJECT

2.1     Physical Characteristics of the Area

Coos Bay is the Estuary of the Coos River located on the western slope of
the Oregon Coast Range. It is the largest natural harbor between Astoria
and San Francisco. The 13-mile long bay has an average width of 1,200
feet at low tide and is tributary to 820 square miles. Charleston is
located at the mouth of the South Slough in the lower Coos Bey estuary.
The Charleston navigation channel leads from the Slough, an area which
drains 29 square miles, to the main Coos Bay channel at mile 2 (fig. 2-1).


2.1.1    General

Tides

Coos Bay has diurnal inequality of tides, typical of the Pacific Coast
of North America. The tidal range, from mean higher high water (MHHW) to
mean lower low water (MLLW), is 6.7 feet at the bar and 6.9 feet at the
city of Coos Bay. Extreme high tide, estimated, is 10.5 feet above MLLW;
extreme low water, estimated, is 3 feet below MLLW. All water depths
and elevations in this report are referred to MLLW.


Depths

The broad area north of the present breakwater to the Coos Bay navigation
channel is at elevation zero to minus 5 feet. It is a dynamic area, with
the elevations of shoals and spits changing seasonally. A shoal extend­
ing north approximately from the end of the present breakwater has been
an identifiable but unstable feature since the time of the earliest
surveys in the late 1800's.

Salinity

Salinity measurements of water samples taken at Charleston gave /alues
from 9.7 to 33.0 parts per thousand (p.p.t),' with most values falling in
the range 25 to 30 p.p.t.  (Queen and Burt, 1955). Coos Bay has been
identified as well-mixed, except during the months of January, April,
June, and November, when it is partly-mixed (Percy, at al_, 1974, p. 51).

Topography

Changes in relative sea level during the Pleistocene Epoch have been
given topographic expression in the area. Sediment eroded from the Coast
Range was deposited in the littoral system during sporadic emergence
of the coastline. As a result, wave-cut terraces are identified at
elevations 50, 250, 500, 1,000, and 1,500 feet. South Slough and
Charleston Channel are separated from the Pacific Ocean by a ridge
generally 400 feet high consisting of shale and sandstone. Sea level
has risen during the last 15,000 years, partially drowning the coast and
creating a drowned river mouth type of estuary at Coos Bay.

2.1.2   Hydraulics

Waves

During the winter, seas greater than 8 feet and swells greater than 12
feet occur from the westerly directions about 20 and 30 percent of the
time, respectively. In the summer, northerly winds produce short period
waves, often as high as 1 to 2 feet, in the Charleston Channel and small-
boat basin. The summer wave pattern is shown on figure 2-2. Winter storm
waves from the ocean are responsible for most of the damage at the boat
basin. Waves approaching Charleston, after being diffracted through the
entrance, are refracted in the shoal area north of the breakwater. These
waves are diffracted around the end of the breakwater. Waves impinging on

2.1.3   Sediment Transport

The sediment making up the spit, shoals, and bottom in the area of the
Charleston breakwater enters the area predominantly from outside the
Coos entrance. This conclusion is based on the sediment description,
the spatial distribution of sediment characteristics, sediment transport
mechanisms acting in the area, and historical changes in the topography.
The rate of sediment transport into the Charleston Channel showed a great
increase in the period 1970 to present, as compared to the period 1961
through 1969, as shown by dredging records. Over the long term, the
channel has been maintained to approximately the same area in cross-
section; however, since 1973, shallower depths have been experienced in
the channel than in previous years. To maintain the cross-sectional
area, an average of 22,000 cubic yards per year were removed from 1961
to 1969 (fiscal years). At that time the spit was near its maximum size.
During the period 1957 through 1978 (fiscal years) an average of 46,000
cubic yards was dredged annually from Charleston Channel.
Sediment Description


The bottom sediment in the Lower Jarvis Range (approximately R.M. 6
through R.M. 8) to the entrance is medium sand with shells present. The
possible origin is thought to be beach sand. Proceeding up the bay, the
material decreased in size to that of silt in the Smith Mill to Marshfield
ranges (R.M. 14, R.M. 22). Arneson (1975) reported finding marine
sediments to predominate to about 10 miles (16 kilometers) from the
entrance. Beyond that the sediment was transitional between marine and
fluviatile before grading into fluviatile sediment at approximately 12
miles (20 kilometers) from the mouth.

Sediment samples collected by the Corps at Coos Bay since 1970 show that
although the statistical values of the size distribution vary with time,
the bottom sediment is consistently coarser in Coos Bay Entrance and
Coos Bay Range than in Charleston Channel.

Sediment Transport Mechanisms

Hydraulic principles are used to interpret transport mechanisms from the
spatial distribution of sand sizes. When a sediment-laden flow enters
an estuary and local velocity and turbulence gradually decrease, the
coarser fractions of the load are the first to be deposited. The
progressive decrease in sediment size from the mouth toward the river
deposited sediment zone indicates decreasing influence of tidal currents
and wave-induced turbulence on the hydraulics of the system, and a direc­
tion of transport from the mouth upstream for about 10 miles in the Coos
system.

The mechanisms responsible for sediment movement from the entrance area
to Charleston are waves, wind, and tidal currents. Waves are considered
the dominant mechanism in forming deposits north of the Charleston
breakwater and calculations have shown that shoaling waves, in combination
with tidal currents, are capable of transporting much more sediment in
the Charleston area than are tidal currents alone.

Ocean waves traveling into the Charleston area are refracted in the
shallow water north of the breakwater. The eastward-directed component
of wave energy is responsible for the dynamics of the sand spit, as well
as the shoaling and shifting of the Charleston Channel. This is inferred
from aerial photos which show the spit formed first at the westward end
of the breakwater in 1956 and enlarged to the east. Maximum size was
reached in 1968. Erosion started at the westward end and before disappear­
ing in 1977, the spit consisted only of a small shoal extending eastward
from the east end of the breakwater.
Channel Morphology

Although the channel shoals severely during storms and is periodically
dredged to at least -10 feet, over the long term, it tends to maintain
itself at particular dimensions. Channel cross-section measurements
from surveys dating from 1973 show that the channel has migrated to the
east in the vicinity of the present breakwater. Observations show that
the cross-sectional area and shape of this channel at a specific river
mile tends to remain constant.

2.2     Biological Condition

2.2.1     Introduction

To simplify discussion, the Charleston area has been divided into
three sections: the area north of the breakwater, the Charleston
Channel-small boat basin area, and the South Slough Sanctuary area.
Each of the three areas has differing physical forces affecting it;
each has developed distinctly different biological populations.

Additional and more detailed discussion of Coos Bay estuarine biological
system may be found in the Corps' 1975 publication, "Coos Bay EIS Supple­
ment Background Information."

2.2.2     Area North of the Charleston Breakwater

This area, which is subject to more direct influence from the ocean than
most other bay locations, is constantly altered by intensive wave activity
and weather conditions.

The sand spit located northeast of the breakwater is transient and has
moved from west to east since the late 1960's (see figs. 2-13, 2-14,
2-15). The shift has changed the topography of the spit. As would be
expected, organisms living in this area must be able to adapt to a
changing environment. The area has been the subject of much study by
the Oregon Institute of Marine Biology (OIMB) and the Oregon Department
of Fish and Wildlife (ODFW).

Benthic organisms (those which live most of their lives on or beneath the-
bay bottom) are not very common in this area. For example, a May 1978
survey at -0.5 feet low tide by OIMB sampled a 100 meter transect line
running northwesterly from the east end of the breakwater and found few
benthic organisms. Using a 1/2 meter square sieve box, only a few
Dungeness crab larvae, a worm, a juvenile flounder, a bentnose
clam (Macoma nasuta) , a shrimp, assorted polychaetes, and various sand
lances were found. Similarly, a 25 Hay 1978 survey at -1.7 feet low tide
found few benthic organisms in the area that would be covered by the
proposed breakwater extension. A 220 meter transect line found a few
olive snails (Olivella biplicata) , another snail (Nassarius tossatus) ,
amphipods (Eshaustorius sp.), polychaetes (Nerinedes tossatus) , and
others in the order Capitellidae) , a megalops, a clam (Tellina salmonea) ,
and unidentified sand lances. The most densely populated site sampled
contained 2 olive snails and 102 polychaetes.

The benthic organism of greatest interest to recreationists in this area
is the razor clam (Siliqua patula) . State-wide, razor clam populations in
coastal estuaries are uncommon. Two areas with significant in-bay popu­
lations are Tillamook Bay and Yaquina Bay. About 90 percent of Oregon's
razor clams are found at Clatsop Beach on the northern coast (Link, 1978).



Locations near Coos Bay where razor clams are found include the open
beach areas of Meyers Creek Beach in Gold Beach, Bastendorff Beach south­
west of Charleston, and North Beach and South Beach in Newport. Some of
these populations are short-lived because of beach instability.

Razor clam populations tend to be sporadic both in size and occurrence
because of the species life history. The razor clam matures in its second
year. Spawning occurs during the spring and summer, when water tempera­
tures exceed 55°F. Each female clam ejects six to ten million eggs, to-
be fertilized by a chance meeting with a sperm cell. During this early
portion of the species' life history, mortality is extremely high. Those
eggs that are fertilized become free swimming larvae carried along by
ocean currents and becoming widely dispersed. After 5 to 16 weeks, shells
begin to develop and the clams settle to the bottom and dig into the
sand. About 95 percent die because of inclement temperatures and other
natural causes. The surviving clams grow quite rapidly, reaching a
harvestable size of 3-1/2 inches in one year and 4-1/2 inches by the
next year. After two years the growth rate slows, with the usual maximum
size of five year old clams being 6 inches (Link, 1978).




As was noted earlier, the bulk of the state's razor clam population is
located on the open north coast. In-bay populations are rare; south
coast, in-bay populations are rarer still.
The area to the north of the breakwater has such a population. Razor
clams have been in the area since the late 1960's, but were not sufficient
in number nor large enough in size to harvest until about 1972, and then
only at tides of -1.5 feet or lower. Since 1974, "clammers" have used the
area extensively; the area is now used at tides of -0.7 feet or lower.
As many as fifty people at a time have been observed in the area during
some of the lowest tides. While intertidal populations are accessible to
the sport clammer, razor clams have been found up to a depth of eight
fathoms at the ocean bottom. Clams also live too deep in the sand to
be disturbed by diggers or the surf.

Although the sand spit area was severely disturbed during the 1977 winter
storms, the clam population appears to be as abundant as in previous years
 (fig. 2-17). ODFW personnel annually conduct a creel census of these
species as well as a study of their age composition (table 2-1) (Link,
1978).

The east side of the channel has not been intensively studied biologically.
In August 1970, students from Oregon Institute of Marine Biology examined
the area as a part of an overall ecological study of the Coos Bay estuary
system (Rosenkeetter , et al, 1970). Results from this overall study are
contained in the Coos Bay Deep Draft FEIS Background Supplement.

A summary of their study of the east channel from the submerged jetty
south to the Charleston Bridge is contained below. One place referred
to frequently is Sand Point, which is north of the bridge (Figure 2-18).
The substrate from the bridge to Sand Point is a mud-to-sand gradient.
North of Sand Point is a hard sandstone base overlain by sand and mud
with many tide pool locations.

The dominant algal species in these areas seemed to stratify with tidal
level and substrate type. Nereocystis beds existed on rocky substrate
at the lowest level of tide. Phyllospadix was found on sandy mud sub­
strates at mean tide level. Ulva beds were found on flat sandstone-clay
substrates at mean tide levels. Enteromorpha was occasionally discovered
in association with Ulva, especially when Ulva was sparse and the flat
sandstone was covered with a thin layer of sandy mud. Fucus and
Pelvetiopsis were discovered on exposed rock with Fucus dominant in the
splash zone and the upper rocky intertidal and Pelvetiopsis dominant in
the mean and upper mean intertidal.

Populations in the tide pools included many species, especially at mean
and lower tide levels between rocks and boulders on the sandstone-clay
shelf. Two species of sculpin were observed: Enophrys bison (buffalo
sculpin) that feeds upon Ulva, and Artedius fenestralis (padded sculpin)
which feeds upon Ulva and Enteromorpha. Stomach analysis of sculpins
captured off the submerged jetty revealed small dungeness crab (Cancer
magister), shrimp, Clinocardium and other molluscs, clam siphons, and
small fish. Sand lances (Ammodytes tobianus personatus) were found in
the sandy mud off Sand Point.

The red-rock crab (Cancer productus) was observed in the Ulva beds and
dungeness crab in the eelgrass beds. The porcelain crab (Petrolisthes)
was found on a mussel shelf (Mytilus californianus) . The Hemigrapsus
nudus (purple shore crab) and Pachygrapsus (the little square-shelled
rock crab) were found together in sandstone rock crevices in the high
intertidal or splash zone. The dominant hermit crab found in the tide
pools was Pagurus hirsutiusculus.

Nudibranchs in the area included Archidoris montereyensis and Acanthodoris
columbina appearing in brown kelp, and Aeolidia papillosa, Hermissenda
crassicornia, and Dirona albolineata found in tide pools and lower tidal
levels.

2.2.4   The South Slough Estuarine Sanctuary

The South Slough Estuarine Sanctuary was authorized by Section 312 of
the Coastal Zone Management Act of 1972 (P.L. 92-583). A grant was
awarded by the Office of Coastal Zone Management, National Oceanic and
Atmospheric Administration, on a matching basis to the State of Oregon
to acquire, develop and operate estuarine areas such as South Slough to
be set aside as natural field laboratories. The Sanctuary, located from
Valino Island southward(fig2-19), contains many undisturbed tideflats and
wetland areas. Through natural inaccessibility, the slough has remained
virtually untouched as a healthy and productive estuarine system. The
sanctuary provides an isolated habitat for a great variety of algae,
marine invertebrates, birds, mammals, fish and plants (Appendix A). The
sanctuary serves as a nursery area for many fish species and contains
approximately 100 acres of commercial oyster culture.


The immediate sanctuary vicinity is used for scientific and archeological
research, education and nature interpretation, and low intensity recre­
ation (such as recreational fishing, hunting, wildlife observation, and
aesthetic enjoyment).

2.3     Socio-Economic Characteristics of the Area

2.3.1     General

Charleston is an unincorporated town with an established fishing community.
The fishing orientation is immediately apparent, with the boat basin
fringed by fish-processing plants and commercial fishing-supply firms as
the dominant features. The handful of retail stores is clearly related to
and influenced by the fishing industry, both in appearance ana in
merchandise. While some of the stores carry tourist-oriented souvenirs
and memorabilia, most supply fishing gear or food and durable goods for
the local residents. The area has a small motel, several mobile home
courts, one tourist-oriented restaurant, and several cafes and taverns.

Across the bay lies Barview, a largely residential area with some strip
commercial development along the highway. Barview merges into the
community of Empire, which borders on the City of Coos Bay. Except for
the area immediately across the bridge from the boat basin, the Barview-
Empire area has fewer outward signs of a fishing orientation.

2.3.2    History

Charleston was founded in the late nineteenth century. The first permanent
non-Indian settler was supposedly a Chinese laborer named Charlie; hence
the name Charlie's Town, eventually Charleston. White settlers were
originally attracted to the area for its fishing and logging resources.
Some farming was done in the South Slough area, and a school and church
were established around the turn of the century.

In 1924, the south jetty was constructed, beginning an era of growth in
Charleston. This also coincided with the population movement from the
South Slough to the area that is now Charleston. A rope-pulled ferry
originally linked Charleston with what is now Barview. In 1909, a
permanent bridge was constructed across the bay. Due to weakening by the
boring Teredo clam, the bridge was replaced in 1930 and again in 1934. The
mid-1920's marked the development of several retail stores, some of which
are currently still in operation. In the 1920's, the post office, cafes,
and taverns were established.

The histories of fishing and fish processing in the Charleston area are
closely intertwined. Salmon, particularly spring chinook, was the first
important commercial species. During the 1920's, greater varieties of
fish were landed commercially, including crab and various bottom fish.

Fishing in Charleston increased during World War II, when large numbers of
commercial fishermen came to the area to catch sharks (for their liver)
and cod (for cod-liver oil) to be used by the U. S. Army. Soon after
the war Charleston began to develop its boat-building industry.
As fishing became more diversified, the types of boats changed. Old-time
residents of Charleston remember when diverse crafts, such as McKenzie
River boats, would fish for salmon. The increasing importance of bottom
fish and, more currently, shrimp has led to a dramatic increase in tht
size of boats. Almost all the commercial boats in the outer basin in the
Charleston Marina are more than 30 feet long. Shrimpers, mainly double­
riggers, and bottom-fish trawlers are the largest boats in the basin.

The first fish-processing operation, Hallmark Fisheries, was established
in 1936. It began as a family enterprise, with the men fishing out of
Charleston and the women canning the catch. The processing industry in
Charleston remained limited until the development of the boat basin in
the late 1950's. In 1958, the Seattle Fishermen's Co-op, one of the many
fishermen's co-ops established along the coast to help sustain the price
of salmon, opened a branch in Charleston. The co-op went bankrupt in
1970 due in part to diminished salmon runs and to internal management
difficulties. Point Adams, which purchased the Charleston plant, soon
sold it to Alaska Packers (Del Monte Corporation), the present owner.

During the 1960's, the remainder of the processing plants currently in
Charleston were established. Sally Salmon Seafood opened in 1962, and
both Peterson Seafoods and Union Fisheries Co-op (now Barbey Packing)
opened Charleston plants in 1965. Currently, there are six processing
plants and three receiving stations in Charleston, as well as one
processing plant in Empire. All the plants and receiving stations buy
salmon, crab, shrimp and, when available, albacore tuna. The fish are
either retailed, processed in Charleston, or trucked to other ports for
processing. There are two custom canning processing operations in
Charleston: Chuck's Seafoods and Sally Salmon Seafoods. Other Charleston
processing plants include:  (1) Alaska Packers, which processes shrimp,
crab, and salmon; (2) Barbey Packing, which processes ground fish, salmon,
and crab; (3) Hallmark Fisheries, which processes shrimp and bottom fish;
and (4) Peterson Seafoods, which processes bottom fish, shrimp, crab,
and salmon. Eureka Fisheries in Empire processes ground fish and shrimp.
The receiving stations are Bandon Fisheries, Crescent Fisheries, and
Tom Lazio Fish Company.

Except for one family-owned plant (Peterson Seafoods), most of the larger
processing plants have undergone repeated ownership changes. Many are now
owned by large corporations that have other plants in various locations
on the Pacific Coast.

Most fish-processing operations in Charleston appear to be fairly healthy.
In 1977, a record of over 12,000,000 pounds of shrimp (Pandalus jordani)
were landed in Charleston, making that port second only to Newport in
shrimp landings. Oregon is second only to Alaska in shrimp landings,
with nearly one quarter of Oregon's shrimp being landed at Charleston. In
response to the boom in shrimping, some Charleston fish processors have
installed new shrimp processing machines. Two processors, Peterson
Seafoods and Barbey Packing, are actively involved in the Pacific hake
project.
The number of employees in the Charleston fish-processing industry has
ranged from fewer than AO in 1958 to a peak of 506 in 1969, reflecting
both th« increased number of fish-processing plants in the area and the
increased processing of shrimp. Shrimp was first processed in the
Charleston area in the early 1960's. Initially, all the shrimp picking
was done by hand, a labor-intensive job generally done by local women.
Shrimp machines, introduced in the late 1960's, substantially decreased
the number of employees needed. Employment in fish processing continues
to be seasonal in nature, with employees working on an "on call" basis.
Interviews in the area indicate that most of the people in fish processing
live in the general Charleston vicinity.

Estimates of the number of commercial fishermen vary greatly. The 1970
census reported only 9 percent of the CCD work force, about 95 people, as
employed in agriculture, forestry, and fisheries.  (Due to the seasonal
nature of fishing, fishermen employment is usually underestimated in the
census). A 1970 Oregon State University study found 115 licensed commer­
cial fishermen in Charleston and another 271 licensed commercial fishermen
in the Coos Bay area (Smith, 1971). The study did not differentiate
between full- and part-time commercial fishermen. A 1976 report estimates
that some 500 Coos Bay area residents (including Charleston) fish
commercially, mainly for salmon (CCD Economic Improvement District, 1976).
Estimates of the current number of commercial fishermen range as high as
620.

Historical trends on the number of fishermen in the Charleston area are
also scarce. Local residents remember a great increase in the number of
fishermen during World War II. The increased number of fishermen helped
provide impetus for the construction of the boat basin.

The Charleston boat basin was built in two phases. A construction bond
issue was raised in 1955, providing funds for the outer basin to be
constructed over the next two years. By 1958, there were 100 berths
available in the outer basin. By the 1960's, the number of berths had
doubled, filling the outer basin to capacity. There was still a demand
for additional facilities, and in 1966 the Port of Coos Bay constructed
a new 10-acre inner basin.

Currently, there are over A80 boats with permanent moorages in the basin.
Approximately 30 percent of the crafts are sport boats and 70 percent are
commercial vessels. The deeper outer basin houses 2A0 boats. These boats
are almost entirely commercial and are upwards of 30 feet in length, with
some trawlers ranging above 60 feet. The inner basin, which houses about
240 boats, has a slightly greater percentage of sport than commercial boats.
The boats in the inner basin are considerably smaller than those in the
outer basin. About half of the 15 charter vessels operating out of
Charleston are berthed in the inner basin.
The boat basin has an additional 80 berths, which are rented on a part-
time basis to smaller crafts during the summer. The nonpermanent mooiage
slots, generally undesirable spots near the bank, are used during the
winter by shrimp and crab boats which tie up horizontally along the piers.
Including the nonpermanent moorages, there were nearly 560 berths in the
Charleston basin in 1977. The boat basin is currently at capacity, with
a waiting list of 280 boats, about 60 percent of which are commercial.

2.3.3   Socio-Cultural Background of Charleston

Fishing Culture in Charleston

Charleston is clearly a commercial and, to a lesser extent, a sport­
fishing area. The boat basin is the dominant visual feature in the town
and plays a dominant role in the lives of most of the inhabitants. As in
most traditional fishing communities, family ties appear to be very strong
in Charleston. Despite the large number of "newcomers" to the field,
fishing is still a profession that is handed from father to child. All
the fishermen and fishermen’s wives interviewed indicated a strong
allegiance to the Charleston area and an identification of Charleston as
their home. This is true both of oldtimers and of relative newcomers to
the area. People seem to have a strong sense of a separate identity of
"Charleston." In many people's minds, Charleston includes that part of
Barview immediately adjacent to the bridge, because that area includes
many old fishing families as well as a fish processing plant and some
fishing-related businesses. The separate physical and social identity of
Charleston seems to be generally recognized by residents of the greater
Coos Bay area.

Due to its small size, the social organizations in Charleston are informal.
The one significant formal social organization in the town was the school
district. Many residents mentioned the loss to community life felt when
the Charleston School District was annexed to the Coos Bay District ten
years ago. The school district had served as a unifying structure, often
bringing together such divergent groups as fishermen and fish processors
for a common cause. Several people mentioned the community work parties
occasionally held at the school before the district's annexation.

After the school district's dissolution, there remained no formal unifying
social organization in Charleston. Today most of the social organizations
are along vocational lines, such as All Coast Fishermen's Association
and Fishermen's Wives Association. The Charleston chapters of both those
organizations are extremely active. Informal gatherings at the cafes and
taverns in Charleston and attendance at a small church are also an
important part of the community social structure. The fishing community
in Charleston is tightly knit and appears to keep a certain amount of
social distance from the nonfishing segment of the community. The
University of Oregon Institute of Marine Biology students and part-time
personnel are generally seen as outsiders lacking sensitivity to the
economic needs of the fishing community.

The fishermen themselves distinguish between commercial fishermen, part-
time fishermen, and sport fishermen. Most of the commercial fishermen are
local residents. Part-time fishermen consist both of general Coos Bay
area residents and of nonlocal residents who fish for salmon out of
Charleston on weekends and during the summer. Most of the part-time
fishermen own small crafts and are part of the "day fleet," being unequipped
for longer ocean voyages. Additionally, there is a large segment of
primarily nonlocal sport fishermen who fish out of Charleston on small
boats. The local fishing community regard these fishermen with less
respect than they do more serious, commercially oriented fishermen. The
sport fishermen who equip their boats with commercial gear are viewed as
being more interested in qualifying for business deductions on tax sheets
than in catching more fish. Local residents sometimes refer to their
boats as the "Tupperware Fleet."

Most of the local fishermen have larger boats, crabbers, shrimpers, bottom-
fish trawlers, or some combination of dragger, crabber, and trawler.
These boats require a very significant capital investment, costing often
upwards of $100,000 for the larger trawlers.

Local part-time fishermen are either crew members of larger boats or
skippers of small salmon vessels. These fishermen may fish on weekends or
fish daily, returning in the early afternoons to work another job, often
in lumber processing in Coos Bay. Salmon fishermen may also stagger years
of full-time fishing with years of part-time or sport fishing. Because
of the large capital outlay required for combination boats, these options
are generally not available to owners of the larger trawlers.

A significant number of the part-time fisherman are nonresidents. Responses
to a 1977 questionnaire indicated that 40% of the boatowners on the
Charleston Marina waiting list were from the interior areas of Oregon,
particularly Douglas County and the Willamette Valley (Cork, 1977).
While not all are commercial fishermen, local fishermen have observed that
many part-time fishermen live in noncoastal Oregon counties.

One of the striking characteristics of fishing communities, including
Charleston, is the tight family structure. An OSU Study found that a
high percentage of the crabbers and combination salmon/tuna/dragger fish­
ermen came from fishing families (Liao and Stevens, 1975). This is seen
in Charleston, where fishing remains the way of life for most of the
community. Both long-established residents and relative newcomer fisher­
men have expressed the hope that their children become fishermen. Studies
in New England have corroborated the important role that family ties and
occupational succession have in the social structure of fishing towns and
ports (Cork, 1977).
Demography

Since Charleston is not an incorporated city, there are no official ctnsus
estimates of the town’s population. The Charleston Census Division (CCD)
includes the entire peninsula between the Pacific Ocean and South Slough,
including Cape Arago, Charleston, and continuing east halfway to Isthmus
Slough and north of the city boundaries of Coos Bay. Barview, Charleston,
and Empire are included in the CCD. The 1970 census showed the population
for the CCD as 3,230. While figures are not available, the Charleston-
Barview area is probably slowly increasing in population despite housing
shortages. Since 1965, annual population rate increases in unincorporated
areas of Coos County have been over twice that of incorporated areas.

The 1970 census reported nearly 15 percent of the population of the
Charleston CCD as below poverty level, with another 23 percent defined as
"near poor." Half the families in the CCD received under $8,300 annually
in 1969. Information for 1975 (Oregon Department of Human Resources)
indicates that 15 percent of the population of the Charleston CCD is still
below poverty level. Many of these people are in the Charleston-Barview
area. Poorer people are attracted to the region by the lower rents and
by the opportunity for a subsistence livelihood based on farming, fishing,
and miscellaneous lumber and wood processing jobs. The educational level,
as well as income in the Charleston CCD, is relatively low. The 1970
census indicated that 63 percent of the people over 25 years old in the
CCD had less than a high school education.

According to the 1970 census, the median age in Coos County was nearly
29 years old; the median age for the Charleston CCD was slightly greater
than that, somewhat over 31 years old. Nearly 10 percent of Charleston's
population was listed as over 65 years old.

Housing

The 1970 census reported the Charleston CCD as having 1,150 housing units,
approximately 70 percent owner-occupied and 25 percent renter-occupied.
The housing profile for Charleston is somewhat different than for the
entire CCD. The Coos County Draft Element of the Comprehensive Plan
singles out Charleston as having a disproportionately greater number of
homes valued under $10,000. This is an indication of both a lack of
affluence and constraints on new housing starts.

Due to high water tables and hardpan layers, much of the Charleston-Barview
area has severe septic tank limitations. While the recent establishment
of a sewer line is expected to result in a significant increase in new
housing, many areas of Charleston and Barview are still on septic tanks
and subject to building limitations.

According to the County's Housing Element draft, nearly A0 percent of the
renters in the Charleston-Barview area are paying more than 25 percent
of their income on rent. The U. S. Department of Housing and Urban Devel­
opment uses this figure as a determinant of fair rent levels. Although
exact figures are not available, the Charleston-Barview area has some of
the least expensive housing in the general Coos Bay area. Much of this
low-cost, usually poor quality housing is found east of Seven Devils Lake
Road in Charleston and east of the Cape Arago Highway in Barview.

Nearly 13 percent of the homes in the Charleston CCD are mobile homes,
considerably higher than the percentage of mobile homes in the rest of the
county. Mobile homes, a lower-cost alternative for single-family housing,
are found both in mobile home parks, one used predominantly by local
residents, and one on port property, used largely by nonresident sport and
sport-commercial fishermen.

Many of the homes along Cape Arago Highway in Charleston and along the
Bay in Barview, show a fair degree of affluence. Further back from the
highway, houses become plainer, more mobile homes are seen, and many of
the homes are in considerable disrepair.

While there are some vacation homes along the Cape Arago Highway, most of
the homes in the Charleston area are inhabited by local residents.
Housing in both Charleston and Barview is almost exclusively single-family
or mobile home.

Despite the 1970 census listing of a 4 percent vacancy rate, local
residents report a severe shortage of housing. The sewer line extension
should induce new residential construction. Much of this, however, may
be beyond the price range of most current Charleston-Barview residents.

Employment

The 1970 census shows about 1,060 of the residents in the CCD as being
employed, making a participation rate (that percentage of work-age persons
in the labor force) of 51 percent. The participation rate for males was
70 percent, while the rate for females was only 30 percent. Nonurban
areas are generally characterized by a relatively small percentage of
working women. Unemployment rates for women were also higher than they
were for men, 7.5 percent for women compared to 4 percent for men.  The
annual average unemployment rates for both sexes was probably significantly
higher; the 1970 census unemployment rates are calculated based on
employment in April, a month of high fishing activity.

Other than employment in fishing and fish processing, Charleston area
residents generally find jobs in Coos Bay/North Bend. Much of the work
is in lumber and wood-processing industries.

Due to the nature of the fishing and fish-processing industries, employment
in the Charleston CCD is fairly seasonal. Overall employment rates in
Coos County are higher than the state average; in 1976, the average annual
county unemployment was nearly 12 percent.
Public Facilities

The Charleston Sanitation District oversees solid waste disposal in tie
Charleston area. In July, 1977 a sewer extension from Coos Bay through
Barview into Charleston was completed. There are currently nearly 270
sewer hookups in Charleston, including the commercial and industrial
establishments in the marina area. The Sanitation District has no
current plans for further sewer line extension in Charleston.

Before the construction of the sewer line, Charleston had severe building
restrictions due to the poor septic tank suitability of the area. Septic
tank failures and poorly functioning drain fields have resulted in the
pollution of some of the area's private water sources. Currently, much
of the residential housing, particularly that off the highway and main
street, is still on septic tanks and subject to the same limitations.

Except for some residences on private wells, most of Charleston obtains
water from the Coos Bay-North Bend Water District. Pony Creek and the
North Spit sand dunes are the district's primary water sources. Recently
purchased water rights on Fourth Creek, Tarheel Reservoir, and Joe Ney
Creek serve as back-up supply for use in the. event of another drought
(Seton, Johnson, and Odell, 1977). Water supply will not be a limiting
factor on nonindustrial development in the Charleston area.

Land Use and Ownership

The most intensive land use (fig. 2-20) in Charleston is found around the
boat basin and along the channel from the basin to the Cape Arago Highway
Bridge. Most of the marine industrial and commercial developments are
concentrated in these locations. The Port-owned property behind the basin
houses many fishing-related ancillary services including a marine
electronics store, a tackle shop, boat storage areas, restrooms with
shower facilities, parking for cars and boat trailers, and Marina and
Fishermen Marketing Association offices. Also on Port land is a mobile
home/trailer park.

Most of the fish processing plants and receiving stations are located on
the Charleston side of the channel. Two are located on the east side of
the bay, one in Empire, and one adjacent to the Cape Arago Highway Bridge.

Non-marine commercial development is found around the basin area, along
the main street and along the Cape Arago Highway, both adjacent to and
immediately west of the bridge. Two tourist-oriented establishments, a
motel and a restaurant, are located behind the basin area. General stores,
eating establishments, fish retail shops, and cafes/taverns are the other
major commercial establishments in the area.

Residential development is mixed in with the marine industrial/commercial
uses along the channel. Homes are also located along and off the main
street in Charleston, and along and off the Cape Arago Highway on both
sides cf the channel. Scattered residences are found in the hills both
behind Charleston and overlooking the South Slough.

Land ownerships (fig. 2-21) in Charleston include the Federal Government
(U. S. Coast Guard), the Port of Coos Bay (marina area and some adjacent
land holdings), the State of Oregon (predominantly the University of
Oregon), School District #9, and private ownerships. Corporate ownerships,
primarily lumber companies become predominant in the timbered hillsides
away from the highway. Land development corporations, many from out of
state, also own sizable tracts of land in this area.

3.3     Statewide Planning

In 1973, the Oregon Legislature adopted a Statewide Land Use Planning
Act (ORS 197) establishing the Land Conservation and Development
Commission (LCDC). The commission was directed to formulate and adopt
statewide planning Goals and Guidelines, which were to be applied and
implemented at the local level through comprehensive plans. County and
local comprehensive plans currently are being formulated and/or updated
to conform to the statewide Goals and Guidelines. Local jurisdictions
are legally required to adhere to the statewide Goals and Guidelines.
Local jurisdictions are legally required to adhere to the statewide
Goals in their planning process. The statewide Guidelines, while not
mandatory, are strong directives on implementation of the goals.
Oregon currently has 19 adopted Goals and Guidelines (table 3-2). The
first two are procedural, emphasizing public involvement and the overall
planning process. Goals 16 through 19 relate specifically to coastal
resource management and part of Oregon's Coastal Zone Management Program.

Of particular pertinance to the proposed action is Goal 5, Natural Re­
sources; Goal 6, Air/Water/Land Resources Quality; Goal 8, Recreational
Needs; Goal 9, The Economy of the State; and Goal 16, Estuarine Resources.

Goal 5 , Open Spaces, Scenic and Historic Areas, and Natural Resources,
calls for the protection of natural and scenic resources and the conser­
vation of open space. The proposed action will result in the loss of some
clam beds, a natural resource of the Charleston area, while creating an
area suitable for new clam beds to establish. Following the implementation
Guidelines of Goal 5, the Corps action is being coordinated with the Ore­
gon Department of Fish and Wildlife to minimize impacts as much as possible.

The proposed action may also cause some temporary conflicts with Goal 6.
which calls for the maintenance and improvements of the water land resource,
and air quality of the state. During the construction phase of the pro­
posed breakwater modification, there would be some increase in noise and
air pollution from construction vehicles. There would also be a temporary
increase in water turbidity. Specifications in Corps contracts will mini­
mize these impacts and applicable water quality certifications will be
obtained from the State of Oregon prior to construction.

Goal 8 . Recreational Needs, is concerned with satisfying the recreational
needs of Oregon's residents and of visitors. The proposed action may
enhance the availability of some popular recreational clam beds, which
are used by both local residents and visitors. The action may also help
maintain the recreational opportunities of the Charleston Boat Basin by
protecting the vessels from extreme surge and shoaling problems.

improvement of the state's economy. Economic growth and activity are to
be emphasized "in areas which have underutilized human and natural resource
capabilities." The planning guidelines strongly emphasize the expansion

ing the local and regional economies. Since the proposed action could

existing industries based on a significant natural resource (fish) in an

rules allow for both major and minor navigation improvements. The Corps'
proposed action in Charleston is within the boundary of the Administrative

4.0     ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACTS OF THE PROPOSED ACTION

4.1     General

The proposed extension is intended to permanently replace a sand spit that
existed adjacent to the Charleston Breakwater until 1977. That spit
reduced the surge problems in the boat basin and protected a portion of
Charleston Channel from shoaling. The shape of the expected sand spit
would be changed slightly from that of former conditions. The naturally-
deposited spit at its maximum size in 1968 extended about 750 feet north
of the breakwater (figure 4-1). The breakwater extension is expected to
cause a spit to form in the shape shown in figure 4-2. The deposit will
extend 800 feet north of the breakwater, but will not be permitted to
extend into the Charleston Channel.

4.2     Hydraulics

4.2.1     Tidal Hydraulics

The Corps of Engineers Waterways Experiment Station (WES) Implicit Flooding
Model was employed to evaluate impacts on tidal hydraulics of Coos Bay
below Empire, including South Slough, caused by constructing the extension
and the groin. Results showed changes in the hydraulics would be confined
to the vicinity of the structures and there would be no significant changes
in the tidal prism, height, or current velocity at any other place in
Coos Bay or South Slough.

The Corps' model is a finite difference model. Input data included tide
elevations at the ocean boundary (Cape Arago gage) and river boundary
(Empire gage), and bottom elevation and hydraulic resistance within grid
cells. The model was verified by comparing measured tide heights with
computed values for seven other locations in the estuary, and by comparing
measured velocities with those computed by the model for ten locations in
Coos Bay and South Slough. Those comparisons showed that the model's
overall ability to simulate the tidal hydraulics is good. A poor comparison
in the upper reaches of South Slough may be due to a lack of detailed
topographic data in the mudflat area and the size scale of the computa­
tional cells.

After model verification, the breakwater extension was coded into the
model, and a second run made.  By comparing velocity and tidal height
values computed in the two runs, changes in hydraulics were predicted.
Alternative plans were similarly analyzed. Except in the vicinity of
the planned structures, the model showed essentially no changes in velo­
city, tide height, or time history of discharge anywhere in the estuary
that would be caused by the planned structures.

4.2.2   Waves

Diffraction studies, using wave periods of 11, 13, and 17 seconds, showed
that an 800-foot extension would decrease the height of ocean storm waves
expected to arrive at the Charleston Channel by a factor of at least 0.25
by the time waves traveled to the end of the present breakwater, and should
provide sufficient protection to boats moored in the boat basin. Waves
have been observed to reflect from the shoreline east of the planned exten­
sion into the boat basin as well as toward the Coast Guard facility located
northwest of the breakwater. The extension and the sand it would trap
would diminish the reflected wave activity. The extension is oriented so
that waves arriving from the ocean would be reflected onto the existing
breakwater. After a second reflection, the energy content of those waves
would be negligible. The possibility of increased wave height due to addi­
tion of incident and reflected waves has been incorporated in the design
for extending the breakwater and raising the crest elevation.
After a spit has formed adjacent to the breakwater, waves would be refracted
onto it and reduce the wave energy progressing past the extension. Waves
then wouLd not be able to break on the breakwater or the extension.

Until the time when a sand spit has formed adjacent to the breakwater,
waves entering the "corner" formed by the extension and the breakwater
would experience an increase in wave height as the incident and reflected
wave combine. Mass transport by the deformed waves will produce a slight
increase in mean water surface elevation. The height of the breakwater
necessary to prevent damage-causing overtopping is determined to be +18
MLLW.

Southerly waves generated in Coos Bay in the summer, shown in figure 2-2,
presently are a problem in the small boat basin. The breakwater extension
would provide an energy-absorbing boundary and reduce the amplitude of
those waves slightly by the time they arrived at the small boat basin. If
the groin opposite the extension is constructed, it would act to decrease
wave energy approaching the boat basin by providing a gap through which the
waves must diffract.

4.3   Sediment Transport

Extending the present breakwater northward would block the direct path of
the sand into the channel and Induce a spit to form again adjacent to the
breakwater. That conclusion is reached by considering the sediment source
and method of transport, discussed in section 2.1, as well as the following
observations.

Extreme shoaling of Charleston Channel occurs during the short period when
storm waves enter through the Coos Bay entrance. Shoaling in Charleston
Channel increased greatly when the sand spit began eroding. Observations
Indicate the spit acted as a temporary trap for sediment being transported
under waves into the estuary. That sediment was later eroded from the spit
and some was deposited in the channel. The extension would be effective
in stabilizing the area.

The amount of sediment moving into the north Charleston area would be un­
changed by the proposed structures and sand is expected to fill the area
formed by the breakwater and the extension. The expected final form of
the spit thus created is shown on figure 4-2. The time period before final
form is reached is expected to be five to ten years. After the topography
became adjusted to the structure, sand would then be available to move past
the extension.

The first phase of construction would consist of extending the present
breakwater to the north and raising the present breakwater crest elevation.
If a deposit forms on the channel side of the end of the extension and
tends to push the channel out of project alinement, a groin would be con­
structed on the east side of the channel to maintain the correct alinement
(second phase of construction). There would be some sand accretion around
the groin, but not as much as is observed in river conditions.
' With the breakwater extension in place, the sedimentary regime in
  Charleston Channel is assumed to return to that existing before the sand
  spit began eroding. When the sand spit was stable, the channel was inly
  moderately bent and caused relatively few problems for users. The
  volume of material dredged from the channel averaged 22,000 cubic yarus
  per year while the spit protected the channel, and 46,000 cubic yards per
  year since the project was constructed. It is assumed that under future
  unimproved conditions, the sheltering action of the spit would not be
  realized again. The average annual dredging requirements under such con­
  ditions are estimated to be 60,000 cubic yards. The extension would

 Thus, less time would be spent by dredges in the area and fewer disposal
 sites will be needed. This would slightly decrease pressure in the area
 to find new upland disposal sites, after the present ones are filled.

 Velocity is predicted to increase at the tip of the breakwater extension.
 At a distance of a few hundred feet northward from the extension tip,
 velocity is practically unchanged from that in base conditions. For a
 short distance beyond the tip of the extension the bottom is expected to
 scour, which will reduce the current speed below the predicted value,
 until a near-equilibrium condition between depth and velocity is reached.
 Scour near the tip of the breakwater extension is expected to affect an
 area roughly circular in shape and about 100 feet in diameter. Four to
 five feet of sand is expected to be scoured from that area, at its deepest
 part.

 The groin, if constructed, will decrease flow velocity near its central
 part and increase velocity near the tip adjacent to Charleston Channel.
 Velocity will be increased only slightly near its shoreward end. The
 preceeding discussion on scour is applicable also to areas of increased
 velocity around the groin. Deposition is expected to occur in zones of
 decreased velocity, both north and south of the groin. However, the
 Charleston groin would not be shore-connected so its sediment trapping
 ability would be less than if it were built out from the shore.

 4.4   Biological Impacts of the Proposed Project

 The proposed project has a number of environmental impacts which would
 occur during the different phases of construction.

 At the start, the impacts would be construction related: stone placement
 by a crane or end dumping by trucks. Since the area around the site is
 shallow, the use of land-based equipment is expected. Stones may be
 stockpiled in the port parking lot adjacent to the site. Construction is
 expected to last at least 5 months, during which traffic congestion on the
 breakwater and in the Charleston Boat Basin area is expected. This will
 also create higher noise levels in the area, plus an increase in air
 pollution from the trucks.
Most bird populations which feed in the project vicinity would be dis­
turbed by construction activity and leave the immediate area for the
duration of the work. They should return after the proposed project is
completed.

While the breakwater is being raised in elevation, there would be a con­
flict between construction activity and maintenance of the traffic flow
to and from Lazio Receiving Station and Point Adams Fish Packing Station.
This problem has been discussed with the stations' managers and construc­
tion activity will be planned to avoid serious traffic delays.

Breakwater construction would require at least 10 truck loads a day of
quarry stone. As each increment of the extension is constructed, aquatic
organisms would be covered and their habitat permanently altered. The
extension and the built-up breakwater, when complete, would cover approx­
imately 3 acres of sandy habitat and create 0.4 acres of intertidal rocky
habitat (above 0.0 feet MLLW). This habitat would encourage establishment
of species similar to those inhabiting jetty areas, such as rockfish,
surfperch, greenling, sculpins, anemones, mussels, barnacles, and attached
algae. Rockfish and sculpins are predatory on juvenile salmonids, so some
increased predation may occur. An increase in these populations also
leads to an increase in food supply available to bird species feeding in
the area, such as the common loon, horned grebe, Western grebe, brown
pelican, double crested cormorant, Brandt's cormorant, pelagic cormorant,
belted kingfisher, and various gulls.

Fishing off the extension for rockfish, surfperch, and greenling may also
occur as populations increase.

Since razor clams are recreationally harvested in the area, the effect of
the project on existing populations has been carefully considered. The
extension would not cover an area of dense razor clam populations. The
sand spit further to the north and west of the extension contain most of
the clams that are recreationally harvested. Sand is expected to accrete
west of the extension, eventually creating a sand spit. The sand spit
would provide an estimated 12 acres of intertidal sandy habitat. The
spit, itself, is expected to contain approximately 11 acres of sand above
MHHW when it has grown to maximum size. This point is expected to be
reached in 10 years, and sand buildup will be gradual. Since razor clam
populations survive ocean bar conditions in which sand periodically builds
up in one area and erodes in another, it is expected that most of the
clams existing in the area at present will survive, due, in part, to their
feeding habits. Razor clams may stay as much as 4 feet below the bottom
surface for as long as 3 weeks before rising within 8 inches of the
surface to feed on plankton. They extend their necks the remaining
distance to the water level, thus staying surrounded by soil.

There is an excellent chance that a razor clam population will develop on
the accreted area, since larval clams are carried by ocean and bay
currents. The waves that would carry the sand to create the beach would
also carry the clam larvae. Subtidal populations of clams in the bay,
which are presently out of reach of recreational clammers, would supply
that larvae. After a year, these clams would grow to a harvestable size
of 3—^5 inches. Since the accreted area would have easier clammer access
(being closer to vehicle parking) and be exposed more low tides than the
sand spit area to the north, this sandy beach may be extensively used
within 5 years after the extension is built.

As the bottom elevation changes more area will be exposed during tidal
action, which will increase the aquatic food supply exposed to birds
feeding in the surf, such as sanderlings, black turnstones, surf scoters,
surfbirds and Northern phalarope.

JJased on model study results, water velocities will increase at the end of
the extension. A bottom depression approximately 100 feet in diameter and
four feet deep may be scoured at this point. This may create an incon­
venience to clammers, since they may either have to avoid the area or wear
chest waders to cross it. It should be possible to use an alternate route
pn the east side of the breakwater, where bottom elevations are expected
to be higher, to reach the concentrated razor clam populations.

Placing of quarry stone will impact fish and other free-swimming
aquatic organisms.  Some of these will sense vibrations in the area in
time to leave, but others may be covered by the stone. The impact is not
expected to be significant.

The placement of stone will create no significant turbidity, since
relatively clean quarry stone (no dirt or toxic chemicals) will be placed
on the sandy substrate.

Because the toe trench would be excavated and the removed sand placed on
the toe and bedding layers during extension construction, localized
turbidity would occur throughout the construction period. Although the
material is clean sand, there would be some burial of organisms, some
siltation of filtering animals and some minor food web disruption. This
is expected to be minimal due to the small amount of material involved.

Since less than 32 centimeters per day of sand would be deposited on the
adjacent sandy bottom, some vertical migration of organisms would occur.
Thus, some individuals would survive even though covered by sand. The
sand layer on the toe rock and bedding layer would allow a more rapid
establishment of estuarine organisms than exposed bare rock.

Excavation of the 8-foot trench would remove organisms living to that
depth in the substrate. Most species live within the surface layer
(approximately 12 inches deep). Crab, shrimp, and clam juveniles in
addition to bottom fish and various species of snails and worms are
examples of organisms which would be disrupted or killed by either filling
or excavation activity. Under the surface layer, species diversity and
density sharply decreases. Razor clams, cockle clams, and worm-like
organisms are examples of the animals which would be impacted by work at
this level.
Certain scavenger birds like the glaucous-winged gull and the Western gull
would be attracted by the aquatic organisms exposed by the excavation.

Vibrations from stone placement and other construction activity may bother
both upstream and downstream migrating fish. It is not anticipated that
this will drive any fish out of the area (except those immediately adjacent
to the structure) since the area is already disturbed (by dredging, boat
movement, other construction, fishing, etc.). Fish migration runs should
not be significantly delayed or altered.

The groin would be constructed from a barge in the channel. Similar
impacts would occur in the area as occurred during stone placement for the
breadwater extension. Approximately 0.2 acres of intertidal rocky habitat
will be created.

No rare or endangered species will be impacted by any phase of the project.

A variety of alternative plans were investigated for improving navigation

Technical Criteria

The following technical criteria were adopted for use in developing a
plan:

    a. The selected plan should be consistent with local, regional, and
State land-use plans and goals and guidelines.

    b. The plan should provide adequate channel depth to accommodate
existing boat basin traffic.

      c.   The plan should maintain the channel in usable alinement.

      d.   The plan should reduce adverse wave conditions.

      e.   To be considered, a plan must be technically feasible.


    f. Design criteria, such as rock size, type of armor, elevation, and
cross-section selection, will be based on wave climate at Charleston.

Economic Criteria

The economic criteria which were applied in formulating a plan are as
follows:

      a.   Tangible economic benefits must exceed project costs.

      b.   Th*_ scope of the development is to provide maximum net benefits.

    c. The plan should be the most economically feasible alternative
available that would accomplish the desired objective.
    d. The benefits and cost are expressed in comparable quantitative
economic terms to the fullest extent possible. Annual cost is based on
a 50-year period of analysis and an interest rate of 6-7/8 percent. Fo
interest during construction was included, as the project could be com­
pleted within one year. The annual charges also include the cost of
maintenance.

Environmental and Other Criteria

The following environmental criteria were considered in formulating a plan:

    a. The planning process must involve natural scientists, engineers,
sociologists, and economists.

    b. An assessment and evaluation of the environmental impacts and of
any irreversible and irretrievable commitments of resources which would be
involved in any proposed action will be completed.

    c. Any detrimental impacts on environment must be avoided when possible.
If any detrimental effects are unavoidable, feasible mitigating measures
or tradeoffs will be studied for implementation.

    d. Consideration will be given to public health, safety, and social
well being.

    e. Sufficient information must be provided to interested local, State,
and Federal governments, private groups, and the general public to obtain
their input regarding existing problems, formulation of alternatives,
impact assessment and evaluation.

Without the proposed improvement there would be no change to the tidal
hydraulic processes that now act in the area. That is not to say if
there were no more structures placed in the estuary, there would never
be a change in estuary hydraulics. To the contrary, sediment will con­
tinue to be delivered to South Slough and the rest of the estuary from
its headwaters. Natural processes act to fill estuaries. The better the
flushing of an estuary, generally, the longer is the time before it fills.

Sand entering the Charleston area tends to restrict the flow through
Charleston Channel and, thus, all of South Slough. With no structural
measures taken to reduce channel shoaling at Charleston tidal flushing
would be poorer in South Slough and the sedimentation rate there would
be expected to be higher than would be the case if the channel were
maintained at deeper-than-present depths.

Under present conditions, waves can be expected to continue diffracting
around the breakwater, and reflecting from the shore between Barview and
Fossil Point. This wave activity would continue to cause structural
damage in the boat basin and shore erosion at Charleston, at the Coast
Guard building northwest of the root of the breakwater, and on the shore
east of Charleston Channel.

Dredging records have shown that, while the spit north of the Charleston
breakwater was near its maximum size, sediment was deposited in Charles­
ton Channel at an average annual rate of 22,000 cubic yards. The future
rate of sediment deposition in the channel, is expected to be 60,000
cubic yards annually, based upon dredging records and the assumption
that a sand spit protecting the channel would not naturally form again
(due to the effect of enlarging the Coos Bay entrance channel).

In the present condition the channel sometimes becomes too shallow and
the alinement too tortuous for a hopper dredge to operate. Under these
conditions, a pipeline dredge must be used to maintain the channel,
resulting in a higher cost for dredged material and, often, more serious
environmental costs than if a hopper dredge could be used.

Economic Effects

People interviewed generally agreed that if the surge and channel problems
are not remedied, the boat basin would not be suitable for year-round com­
mercial fishing. Currently, there are very few facilities in the Coos
Bay area that could serve as moorages for part of the fishing fleet. During
the bad winter storms of 1977, the beats in the outer basin were tied up
to any available moorages including fish processing plants and miscellaneous
docks in the upper bay. At that time, as now, there were no formal
facilities that could permanently handle the significant numbers of
larger commercial fishing vessels found in the outer basin of the
Charleston Marina. Although all the fishermen and families interviewed
expressed a very strong desire to stay in the Coos Bay area, all agreed
that adequate facilities did not exist for boat moorages other than the
Charleston Marina.

The City of Coos Bay has some old moorages in the downtown area. Proximity
to the shipping channel makes these somewhat dangerous to use. The moorages
are in considerable disrepair and a plan to renovate them remains dormant.

Two of the fish processing plants, Hallmark Fisheries and Peterson Seafood,
have some moorage spaces. These are generally reserved for the temporary
tie-up of fishing boats serving the processors.

Hanson's Landing, directly upstream of the Charleston Marina, has a total
of about 50 moorage spots on the bay and Joe Ney Slough. The boats
range from 20 feet to 50 feet in length. About half of them
are commercial. Hanson's Landing is currently at capacity and has a
waiting list of about twenty boats. The landing's location upstream of the
Cape Arago Highway drawbridge makes it an undesirable spot for large vessel
moorage. Automobile traffic is severely congested, particularly during the
summer, by frequent opening of the bridge to accomodate hoat traffic.

Currently, the Port of Coos Bay has a permit application pending before
the Army Corps of Engineers to allow the construction of a trawler basin
on the North Spit. Long range plans by the Port include development of
the bottom fishing industry and establishment of the proper processing
plants on the North Spit. While some of the larger trawlers in the
Charleston Marina could conceivably use this basin, it is not being design­
ed for use by small vessels, nor for permanent moorages of many vessels.
The future of the bottom fishing industry in the Coos Bay area and the
outcome of the proposed trawler basin cannot, therefore, be seriously
considered as an alternative for the boats currently moored in the Charles­
ton Marina. The Charleston facilities are being fully utilized by fisher­
men catching shrimp, crab, salmon, bottomfish, etc.

All the major fishing ports along the Oregon and northern California coast
are operating at capacity and have significant waiting lists. Most people
interviewed mentioned Newport as being the most desirable port to them
outside of Coos Bay. Other ports mentioned included Crescent City and
Eureka. The small fishing ports such as Brookings and Winchester have
bars that make entry during winter months all but impossible.
Fishermen, particularly those with stong family ties, would
be very reluctant to relocate to areas outside of Coos Bay.

Failure to solve the surge and channel problems would also have
significant impacts on the fish processing industry. Due to the difficulty
in obtaining permits for construction of new fish processing plants,
particularly in obtaining water-quality permits, it is doubtful
whether the current plants would relocate. Those plants that have
branches in other ports would probably relocate their equipment to those
plants and possibly close down their Charleston operation. Plants that
operate only in the Charleston area would have the alternative of: 1)
trucking fish in from landing stations elsewhere on the coast; 2) using
local landings and greatly curtailing operation; or 3) closing down
operation. Some fish landings, particularly salmon, which is generally
fished out of small vessels, could continue in the Charleston area. The
larger vessels, however, would be seeking other moorages.

The ties between the fishing industry and the fish processing industry are
close. Two to two and a half shrimp boats are generally needed to supply
enough shrimp to run one shrimping machine. Strong but informal agreements
are generally made between processors and boats that will supply them.
Fishermen, particularly shrimpers that relocate to other areas, would thus
have to find processors who could use their catch.

Community Effects

The socio-cultural effects of not remedying the surge and channel problems
in the Charleston Marina would be profound. The owners of the larger
fishing trawlers would be forced to seek moorages elsewhere. Since there
are few adequate alternative moorage spots in the Coos Bay area, many of
these fishermen would ultimately be forced to leave the area, severely
disrupting social ties. Since family networks are an important aspect
in the community, relocation would be a source of great social change in
Charleston.

The community of Charleston itself might become predominantly a summer
fishing area. Vacation of the area by commercial fishing vessels would
create more space for sport fishing and smaller sport-commercial vessels.
The character of the area might become more tourist and sport-fishing
oriented.

A decrease in commercial fishing would have secondary economic impacts.
Marine commercial enterprises may suffer a loss of business. General
commercial establishments would become more dependent on tourism support
by sport fishermen. There would probably be an overall loss of income
to the current residents of the area. Only a small percentage of current
residents would be able to afford new housing.
The recent extension of the sewer line to Charleston is expected to
encourage considerable residential development. An influx of new residents
with no ties to the fishing industry might further dilute the currentl/
pronounced commercial fishing orientation and character of Charleston.


6.3   Structural Alternatives

The following structural alternatives were tested for their hydraulic
effects on the estuarine system using a numerical model. If an alternative
failed to meet either the technical or economic criteria, it was not studied
further r

6.3.1   Alternative 1

Construction of an L-shaped breakwater north of the present breakwater and
three groins on the east side of Charleston Channel was the most extreme
structural modification tested. Although that alternative showed no adverse
hydraulic impacts would result, the plan was rejected for economic reasons;

The top elevation of the 400-foot-long groins and of the 3600-foot-long
breakwater was to be +14 feet. That plan would require about 345,000 more
tons of rock and would cover about 12 more acres of sandy subtidal bottom
than the chosen alternative. In terms of labor and materials, alternative
1 is about $8 million more expensive than the chosen plan. Alternative 1
would have caused a sand spit to form in the area that is now a recreational
clamming area.

6.3.2   Alternative 2

Extending the breakwater 1000 feet northward was analyzed but was rejected
because it would not appreciably decrease channel maintenance costs to
any greater degree than would the 800-foot extension. The 800-foot exten­
sion was the length that provided the minimum amount of wave energy reduc­
tion in the boat basin for safe conditions. The 1000-foot extension would
reduce the wave height of a 17-second wave in the mooring basin only slightly
more than the 800-foot extension would. The 200 feet of additional extension
would require 7,795 tons more rock and would cover about 0.5 acre more
sandy bottom.

6.3.3   Alternative 3

Modeling the extension and one groin on the east side of the channel
showed that plan would be very effective in protecting the channel from
shoaling. Maximum velocity in the channel would be increased by the
presence of the groin. The extension alone may prevent problems of channel
depth and alinement. The channel has the ability to adjust its own shape,
and therefore the flow and sediment transport characteristics. Since
the adjustment of the channel to the presence of the breakwater cannot be
analytically determined and due to the importance of waves in the channel
shoaling problem, the selected project deferred construction of the groin
until actual performance of the extension could be evaluated. The groin
would consist of about 35,600 tons of rock and would cover about one acre
of sandy and rocky subtidal bottom.

6.3.4   Alternative 4

A single groin on the east side of Charleston Channel near the end of the
breakwater was considered as a means of keeping the channel in correct
alinement and to reflect waves away from the boat basin. However, it was
determined that the groin, acting alone, would not reduce the channel
maintenance costs because the groin would not block the sediment from
moving into the channel. The groin would not prevent waves from diffrac­
ting around the end of the present breakwater, which presently causes
much damage in the mooring basin.

6.3.5   Alternative 5

Two groins, in addition to the extension, were shown by the model to pro­
duce more concentrated flow in the channel than would be produced with
only one groin and the extension. That alternative should not impact the
hydraulics outside the Charleston area, but the flow conditions in the
channel produced by the second groin are not considered necessary to main­
tain the channel at project depth and alinement. The second groin would
cover about one acre of subtidal bottom and require about 35,500 tons of
rock.

6.3.6   Alternative 6

Repositioning the channel, with no other structural modifications, would
be only a temporary improvement for navigation. Under the prevailing
dynamic conditions, any dredged area would tend to return to its pre­
dredged condition. A dredging program which would provide a channel to
project dimensions on the existing alinement would require dredging ap­
proximately 20,000 cubic yards annually more than would be dredged under
the "without improvement" conditions. Additionally, benefits due to
reduced surge damage in the basin would not be realized by the dredge-only
alternative. The monetary cost as well as the environmental cost of
dredging and disposing of the shoal material has led to formulating the
plan to structurally protect the channel where it has shoaled the most.
7.0   RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN LOCAL SHORT-TERM USES OF MAN'S ENVIRONMENT
      AND THE MAINTENANCE AND ENHANCEMENT OF LONG-TERM PRODUCTIVITY

As the stone is placed for the breakwater extension, 3.0 acres of sandy
substrate will be covered. The structure will create 0.4 acres of inter­
tidal rocky habitat. Within 10 years an estimated 11 acres of sand above
MHHW would form and about 12 acres of intertidal sandy habitat would
accrete.

Filling of the excavated toe trench would permanently change approximately
10.000 cubic yards below the surface from fine sand to a large stone
composition. Sand is expected to fill around the rock allowing the
surface layers of the toe material to return to preconstruction Conditions.

Groin construction would eliminate less than 1 acre of rocky in-bay
subtidal habitat and replace it with 0.2 acres of rocky intertidal
habitat.

In the short term, there will be increased use of fossil fuels, an increase
in noise levels, and a worsening in air quality during construction, plus
traffic congestion in the Charleston are. Over the long run, the loca­
tional advantages should reduce use of fossil fuels, however.

The increased elevation of the breakwater will prevent overtopping of the
structure during winter storms.

These structures will help maintain Charleston's fishing industry as it
has been in the past by reducing recent channel passage problems and
surge problems in the boat basin. Since navigation conditions should
return to the former conditions, it is possible that there will be
increased pressure to expand the existing boat basin area.
8.   IRREVERSIBLE AND IRRETRIEVABLE COMMITMENTS OF RESOURCES WHICH WOULD
     BF INVOLVED IN THE PROPOSED ACTION SHOULD IT BE IMPLEMENTED

Removal of the stone for the proposed breakwater extension and possible
groin structure from its source(s) at commercial quarries will be
irreversible. Placement of stones will alter the effect of wave action
from the bay entrance and reduce shoaling within the Charleston channel.
A 50-year life is assumed for economic purposes, but the actual life of
the project will be indefinitely longer. Emplacement of the rock would
remove 3.0 acres of sandy bottom habitat and replace it with 0.4 acres
of intertidal, irregular, rocky surface.

Filling of the toe trench would permanently change approximately 10,000
cubic yards below the surface from fine sand to a large stone construc­
tion. Sand is expected to fill around the rock allowing the surface
layers of the toe material to return to preconstruction conditions.


The sand spit which would form west of the proposed extension would con­
tain about 11 acres of sand above MHHW and cause 12 acres of intertidal
sandy habitat to accrete.

Some shoaling would occur both north and south of the proposed groin
structure and would cover approximately one acre of sandy and rocky,
subtidal benthic habitat.

Allocation of labor and capital for construction of the breakwater
extension and possible groin structure will preclude the Investment
of these resources elsewhere. Both gasoline and diesel oil would be
consumed during construction.

As you are no doubt aware, on October 1, 1977, pursuant to provisions
of the Department of Energy Organization Act, the Federal Power Com­

ties were transferred to the Secretary of Energy and the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission, an independent regulatory commission within the
Department of Energy. Reference to the Federal Power Commission on

Energy Regulatory Commission.

on matters affecting the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s
responsibilities. Such responsibilities relate to the licensing of
non-federal hydroelectric projects and associated transmission lines;
certification for construction and operation of natural gas pipeline
facilities, defined to include both interstate pipeline and terminal
facilities; and the permission and approval required for the abandon­
ment of natural gas pipeline facilities.

those areas of concern nor serious conflicts with this agency's
responsibilities if this plan were adopted.
