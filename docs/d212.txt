    1. THE ENGLISH LITERATURE EXAMINATION
                     OF 1951

    The following information in regard to the work of the
candidates at the Grade XIII examination of 1951 in English
Literature is presented in the hope that it may prove helpful to
teachers of English in the secondary schools. It is to be under¬
stood that the allotment of marks to the several parts of the
course and the comments upon the candidates’ answers apply
only to this examination and that neither the scale of valuations
nor the form of questions will necessarily be duplicated in future
examinations in the subject.

    The answer papers were evaluated on the basis of 100 marks
which were divided as follows: Shakespeare—12; Shakespeare
and Modern Drama—8; Modern Drama—8; Longer Poems—19;
Shorter Poems—19; Short Stories and Essays—10; Pride and
Prejudice—12; Sight Poetry—12. The marks assigned are also
shown opposite the individual questions.

I. FORM OF ANSWERS
     It is very important that answers should be properly spaced,
accurately numbered as to parts and sub-parts, and written
legibly. Proper organization of subject matter is often helped by
proper formal organization.

                                1
                                2

     Despite the fact that the question paper seemed somewhat
longer than usual, most candidates completed the answer paper.
Many even added materially to its length by answering all the
options in 2 (a) or (b). While the paper may have required
more reading and understanding of questions than in former
years, the ability to grasp quickly the meaning of question or
instruction is one in which students should be trained and in
which they may expect to be tested in and out of school.

     To avoid being hurried towards the end of an examination,
students should be taught to allot an approximate period of time
to each question and then to adhere to such schedule. They should
be discouraged from writing full answers on the unruled part of
the examination book which they waste time in merely recopying
on the ruled pages.

    A good examination tests more than a mere knowledge of
matter studied in class, and students should be trained in the
writing of formal examinations from the time they enter second¬
ary school. They would then be at a distinct advantage when
faced with the departmental examination in English Literature.

     Students should be taught to pick out the key words in a
question. Practically every word in a well-constructed question
has a bearing on the answer. Such key words are adequate in
Question 2 (a), briefly in 3, in your own words in 3, 4 and 9,
refer to the passage in 4, compare in 5, relative in 7, and leading
in 8.

     Proper analysis of the wording would prevent the laborious
writing of ‘"answers” which do not answer what is asked. Candi¬
dates often fail to distinguish between a summary and a critical
analysis. See Question 7 (b).

     They often do not relate their material to the specific points
raised or they give many details not required. See Question 3.
Having made sure what the question requires, the student should
then develop an answer well-organized and unified, beginning
with a topic sentence which reflects the purpose and meaning of
the question and, if possible, rounded off with a strong concluding
sentence.

     Teachers should continue to stress both these points—a
careful analysis of the question; a thoughtful well-organized
answer. Classroom drill might well be given, especially in the
former of these.

II. ERRORS IN SPELLING

     A maximum deduction of five marks was made on the 1951
paper for mistakes in Spelling. Because of these errors in Spelling,
many students failed to achieve first class honours. All too
frequently candidates failed to pass for the same reason. English
teachers would do well to devote a period or two to the rules of
spelling in the hope that students may become spelling-conscious.
No candidate should misspell words which appear before him on
the examination paper. The Speller used in Commercial classes
is excellent for a spelling review. The spelling of plurals, of
possessives, of various verb forms, and the use of capitals require
special attention. The following words were among those most

III. FAULTY USAGE

    A maximum deduction of six marks was made for faulty
usage on the 1951 paper. Although a general improvement in
expression was noted, there were enough errors to indicate that
the usual stress on correct usage is necessary. Common errors
found in this year’s answers included such incorrect expressions
as: had sank, had of, like he did, who he knew, is when, have
wrote, was showed, he laid down, there is two types, and a chance
of him learning. Then too there were examples of carelessness,
such as, “He is prejudice to her,” “she love Dacy.” Many slang
expressions were also found. Senior students ought not to express
themselves as follows: “slow to get riled up,” “she gave him the
come-on look.” Many answers were marred by run-on, confused

sentences; for example, “To any character who did not play false
with Hamlet he was as congenial as any man could be, but to
anyone who Hamlet did not like because of their low and cheap
dealings, not through stupidity, he was a mean adversary to
talk to.”

Question 1

    Show how the nature of the main character in the play by
Shakespeare which you have studied is revealed in the following
ways:

    (a) by the   attitude of the main character tozvard members
        of his (or her) own immediate family or toward humble
        characters who are under his (or her)    direct authority;
        (6 marks)

    (b) by the speech and conduct of the main character during
        one period of critical emergency. (6 marks)

      Many candidates failed to read this question for the key
words—the     main character,    members of his (or her) own
immediate family, humble characters under his (or her) direct
authority. Lady Macbeth was sometimes chosen as the “main
character.” There was difficulty over “his own immediate family.”
Duncan was sometimes chosen as a member of Macbeth’s im¬
mediate family. Many students included Banquo, Macduff, and
even the witches as humble characters under Macbeth’s control.
This type of error occurred in answers based on the other plays
also.

     The question, however, was well answered on the whole.
Many students stressed the action rather than the quality; for
example, “Macbeth loved his wife” instead of “Macbeth was
affectionate.” Many students were satisfied to mention one trait
of character when several might have been deduced from the one
speech or action.

    (a) (Macbeth chosen)

        At the beginning of the play Macbeth is shown to be
    an ambitious but still very human man. His attitude to his
    wife shows this clearly. He confides all his thoughts and
    plans to her by means of letters. He writes her about every
    event so that she might enjoy his success. This shows his
    love and affection for his wife and therefore shows that he is
    very human.
                                5

        His lack of moral courage is shown by his dependence
    on Lady Macbeth. Before the murder of Duncan, he requires
    her moral courage to bolster his failing courage.

         As the play continues, however, the degeneration of his
    character begins and this is also shown in his attitude to his
    wife. He no longer confides in her or requires her moral
    courage when he commits his evil deeds. Before the murder
    of Banquo he tells her it is better that she should not know
    his plans. He is still dependent on her presence of mind,
    however, since only her quick wit saves him in the banquet
    scene.

        Thus his attitude to his wife reveals his nature.

    (b) (Hamlet chosen)

         The scene where Hamlet fights with Laertes at the end
    of the play is an example of critical emergency. The emer¬
    gency does not arise until the dying Laertes informs the
    wounded Hamlet that “The King! The King’s to blame!”
    At this point Hamlet, realizing he is dying, knows that he
    must kill the king or never again have the chance. Accord¬
    ingly he kills Claudius; finally Hamlet’s revenge has come.

         In this scene Hamlet’s impulsive action and quickness
    of action show up his character. He has been irresolute and
    he “thinks too precisely on the event.” Now the other side
    of his nature is portrayed. He can act quickly when he stops
    thinking too much about the action and its results.

        The fact that Hamlet had to be told who was to blame
    and the fact that he didn’t suspect anything foul during the
    duel indicates Hamlet’s trust in people. Against the good
    quality a very bad one is shown up. Hamlet killed the king
    when he was absolutely forced to do so. This fact shows us
    how weak and irresolute he was. He had a keen sense of
    duty and obedience (to the ghost), but unfortunately he
    seemed incapable of acting according to his thoughts.

        In this last scene we also see Hamlet’s sensible nature.
    He ordered Horatio not to drink poison, so that Horatio
    could tell the people the true story of the tragedy.

     (a) Many candidates wasted considerable time by failing to

    Too many candidates failed to make any comparison what¬

     In (ii) (suspense) many candidates seemed to interpret the
term as meaning a high point of interest or excitement, rather
than a more or less prolonged period of tension, uncertainty, or
doubt. Many good answers were expressed in the form of
questions: “Will the conspiracy be discovered because of Portia’s
disclosures to Lucius?” Others were stated: “We wonder whether
Browning and Elizabeth will get away without discovery.”

     In (iii) (intensity and dramatic significance of the crisis),
some candidates failed to establish clearly the period of crisis
in each play; and many more failed to show that it was a crisis
by tracing either the events of the rising action which preceded
or events in the falling action which followed this turning point.
The following answer obtained full marks:

        (a)  (ii) Shakespeare is a master at creating an element
    of suspense. This art is readily seen in Act V of “Macbeth.”
    Here, the great Macbeth is facing his last obstacle, and
    Macduff, who has become more and more a hero-like man of
    fine, moral standing, is preparing to avenge the murder of
    his family. Shakespeare tells the readers of the approaching
                                7

    army, darts back to the frustrated Macbeth, and returns
    again to the army as it advances slowly. This continuous
    change of scene conveys an atmosphere of intense activity
    and coming disaster. Tension and suspense mount higher
    and higher. The reader sympathizes with Macbeth, admires
    him for his burst of courage, then respects Macduff and
    supports him in the struggle. By this device of many short,
    varied scenes, Shakespeare introduces the element of
    suspense.

          In “The Barretts of Wimpole Street,” this change of
    scene, however, is not the means of creating suspense, nor
    is the suspense as tense as in “Macbeth.” In this play, the
    whole story is of a milder vein, and there is no danger of a
    hand-to-hand combat to worry about. Hence the suspense
    is considerably lessened. The suspense developed here is
    evident by the speeches of the characters. The whole house¬
    hold is under the tyrannical influence of the stern father.
    When the members of the family, that is, Elizabeth and
    Henrietta, show signs of defying the indomitable will of the
    master of the house, a certain amount of tension occurs as
    we worry about the results of this obedience. The conver¬
    sation of the sons and daughters creates a tinge of suspense,
    but it is the time when Elizabeth leaves a note for her father
    and all await his arrival that the suspense reaches its climax.
          Suspense in “Macbeth” is more evident and more intense
    than in the “Barretts of Wimpole Street.” Thus the play
    itself should be more violent and tense.

    (b) In answering this question many candidates failed to
show the significance of their choices in the plot development,

choices. If they did show relationship to the plot, they frequently

     The answers to questions on the Shorter Poems often re¬

        (a) On First Looking into Chapman’s Homer.         This

    that a new world has been opened to him.

    a promise of immortality.

        (c) The poet uses imagery most        effectively in both
    poems.

        "On First Looking into Chapman s Homer”         is an ex¬
    tended metaphor. It is very figurative—

        “Much have I travelled in the realms of gold.
        And many goodly states and kingdoms seen.”

        This is Keats’s figurative way of telling how much he
    has read in the world of literature.

         Homer’s writings are compared to “one wide expanse”
    which “Homer ruled as his demesne.” The sestet is also
    figurative. Keats compares himself to “some watcher of the
    skies, when a new planet swims into his ken” or—“like stout

Question 6

    “When icicles hang by the wall,” “Last May a braw wooer,”
Caller Herrin’, and Trade Winds are well-known songs. Choose
any one of these poems and state three good reasons for its
appeal. (3x3 marks)

     In answering question (6) many candidates failed not only
to give three definite reasons for liking the poem, but also to
present reasons that would indicate the student’s knowledge of
the poem. An example of a poor answer ran as follows: “I like
the poem because it is well-written, has a flowing rhythm, and
is in dialect.”

     The answer printed below is an example of a more thought¬
ful type:

        Trade Winds     has an appeal to the reader. It has a free,
    swinging rhythm, in harmony with the indolent, carefree,
    monotonous life of the tropics. In contrast there is the steady
    beat of the refrain. “The steady trade winds blowing” beats
    in the steadiness of the wind. There is an abundance of
    rhyme -aab- which makes the poem musical.

         Imitative harmony is used effectively. “The soughing of
    the sail,” “the squeaking fiddle,” “the shuffle of many feet,”
    “the long, low croon” give the poem added appeal. Colour
    words appeal to the eye: “the red wine,” “the nutty Spanish
    ale,” “the orange-trees” and “the ghostly palm trees.” The
    numerous images, as evident in the above quotations, appeal
    to the imagination.

Question 7 (First option)

    The weaknesses in the answers to this question were due to
a misunderstanding of the content of Chesterton’s essay,
especially in connection with C. B. Fry, and a tendency to give
a summary rather than an interpretation of the essay in terms of
the question. The following answer was given full marks, 10,
because it deals with ten points including the necessary refer¬
ences to Fry and the battle of Waterloo.

         Chesterton does not believe that there is any connection
    between national greatness and success in athletics. Some
    Englishmen seem to think that the English have a right to
    be superior in athletics, but Chesterton says that athletics
    are individualistic, and just as Barnum’s freaks do not
    represent America, good athletes do not represent their own
    country. Although Indians are not lovers of sport, many
    famous cricket players are Hindus; and although Jews are
    peace-loving, there have been famous Jewish prizefighters.
    The writer thinks that the saying, “The battle of Waterloo
    was won on the playing fields of Eton,” is wrong. He says
    it was won by the strength of the ordinary man and therefore
    on the village green—not on the playing fields of Eton. The
    writer thinks that it is a good sign when most athletes are

    (a) As pictured in the story, Tom was an honest, powerful,

   2. THE ENGLISH COMPOSITION EXAMINATION
                             OF 1951

     The following comments on the Upper School examination
of 1951 in English Composition may be useful to teachers of this
subject. The allotment of marks and the method of marking
used in this examination will not necessarily apply in future
examinations.

    The marks assigned to the various parts of the paper were
as follows:

    The Essay .                                      70 marks
    The Vocabulary .                                  5 marks
    The Grammar .                                    10 marks
    The Appreciation .                               15 marks
                                             Total 100 marks

I. Question 1, The Essay—70 marks

A. Standards
    The following are in part the standards by which the essay
was marked:
     1.   The essay shall be marked on a basis of 70.

     2.   Prejudice should not be allowed to influence the mark.

     3.   An essay that is partly irrelevant shall be penalized up
          to five marks. An essay completely irrelevant shall be
          referred to the committee.

     4.   An essay of exceptional merit, which falls slightly short
          of the required 300 words, shall not be penalized for its
          brevity. An essay that exceeds the required length shall
          not be penalized unless it is wordy. Essays of doubtful
          length may be submitted to the committee.
                                    20

      5.   Over-punctuation or under-punctuation,           aside from
           major errors, should be considered in           the general
           estimate.

      6.   The initial letter of the title, when there is a title, should
           be capitalized.

      7.   Essays should be judged on their positive merits and
           penalized for errors.

      8.   Remember that there are many different kinds of writ¬
           ing, for example, the light familiar style, and the clear,
           forceful, formal style.

B. Grading:

     (a) Passing grades are given for positive merits; less than
         50% is given an essay with serious defects.

     (b) The essay should not be graded on correctness alone;
         the student should have something to say. Occasionally,
         an essay with good content may have been written by a
         candidate enthusiastic about his subject, who, because
         of his enthusiasm, has fallen into minor errors, even into
         major errors. Such an essay should not be penalized
         too severely, if the student has expressed himself well
         in spite of his mistakes.

1. Failures (below 35)
     Three major errors of different types, many examples of a
single major error, or frequent minor errors of different types,
should put the essay below fifty per cent (35 marks), unless there
are compensating merits.  Very defective essays should be marked
below forty per cent (28 marks).

2. Credit (35-41)
    Commonplace,     comparatively free from errors. The essay
passes because there are not enough serious defects to justify a
failing grade; it has not enough positive merit to place it in an
Honour Class.

3. Honours

     In order to deserve honours the candidate must show that
he can write reasonably good English and that he has some power
of organizing his ideas.
                                21

(a) Third Class (42-45)
     This essay resembles the credit essay in being common¬
place, but it reveals more power of organization than a credit
essay. It resembles a second-class essay in having some merit
in either content or style, but it lacks the interest of the second-
class essay.

(b) Second Class (46-51)
     Definite merit in content or style, competently handled and
interesting.

(c) First Class (52-70)
      Merit in both content and style. If an essay seems to be
first-class in spite of some errors which would place it in a
lower grade, refer it to the committee. A thoughtful essay written
in a plain, straightforward style shall be given first-class hon¬
ours. An essay to be worth this grade should interest the reader.
(1) Attractive, meritorious, 52 to 58 marks; good content, some
consciousness of word values, effective sentence structure. The
candidate has something to say, and shows definite ability in
expressing himself.
(2) Excellent in both content and style, 59-64 marks; for
example, comprehensive and intimate knowledge of subject
matter, freshness of thought, skill in arrangement, command of
sentence structure (variety, emphasis, smoothness), conscious¬
ness of word values.
(3) Distinguished, 65-70 marks: marked individuality in content
and style. Any such outstanding essay should be shown to the
committee.

C. General Comment
1.   The Essay Topics

    Though the topics were quite suitable for the examination,
the development by students was often disappointing.

(a) A Hallowe’en I’ll never Forget.
     Many students received poor marks because they tried to fit
irrelevant material into this topic: the incidents used frequently
bore only the most superficial relation to the idea of Hallowe’en.
Other students used melodramatic, implausible incidents.

(b) Portrait of a Grandmother (or Grandfather)
      Many of the portraits were too sentimental or too conven-
                                  22

tional and presented unrealistic pictures. Nevertheless many
first class essays were written on this topic by students having
an eye for realistic detail and a gift of vivid expression.

(c) What Music Means to Me

    Too often essays on this topic were simply a jumble of
unrelated ideas. The best efforts were made by those who com¬
bined knowledge and appreciation with imaginative expression.
Many students failed to make explicit reference to various types
of music, and to particular musical compositions.

(d) Learning to Swim

     There were a great many poor essays written on this topic
by students who had little to say and said that little badly. Many
essays were poorly organized and contained little valid inform¬
ation. Most of the essays on swimming were dull expository
attempts at explaining the progressive steps in learning to swim,
from the dog-paddle to the more masterly strokes.

(e) The Value of the United Nations Organization

     Deplorable ignorance was evident in the treatment of this
topic. Students confused the U.N.O. with the League of Nations
and had only a vague idea of the real value of the U.N.O. Essays
were frequently dull, trite, and padded. Many scholarship essays
on this topic however, were excellent both in content and style.

(f) Flying Saucers

     Incompetent writers uttered unhappy echoes of fantastic
pseudo-scientific material, while others showed skill and ingen¬
uity in their treatment of the topic. Occasionally an examiner
found a genuinely humorous essay on this topic.

The chief reasons for failure:

      1.   poor command of sentence structure,
      2.   lack of knowledge about the topic,
      3.   inability to plan the essay,
      4.   inability to develop full paragraphs,
      5.   mediocrity of style—dullness, drabness, use of hackneyed
           English.

2 (a) Pujictuation

    Teachers should give attention in composition classes to the
                                23

proper use of the comma and failure to use the apostrophe. The
comma blunder was very frequently encountered. Punctuation
generally was weak.

(b) Agreement

    Students made many errors in the agreement of verb and
subject.

(c) Pronoun reference

     Every possible kind of error was made in the use of pronouns.
Many pronouns were used for which there was no clear relation
to an antecedent at all. The use of the unrelated “which” and of
the vague “this” was frequently met.

(d) Slang and Colloquialisms

     A great many students are incapable of distinguishing
between the spoken and the literary language. It would seem
advisable to discourage students from using any expressions of
dubious usage. Much use could be made of a comprehensive list
of intolerable expressions. On the other hand students should be
told that a stilted, wooden, or flowery style is almost as bad as
a slangy one.


(e) Good Taste

     Students should be warned that essays, especially when
intended as humorous, must be in good taste. Otherwise the
essay loses marks. A number of Hallowe’en essays were deficient
in taste as well as style.


(f) Ideas and Style

     Few students seem capable of expressing an idea with vigour
and force.     Individualism in style — excepting certain wild
eccentricities—is notably absent from the essays. Many students
are still being led down the garden path of flowery, overwritten
description. Occasionally students out-Herod Herod. Teachers
should strive to have their students write in a clear, sincere,
lively style.

(g) Irrelevancy and Twisting

      Some students still try to fit prepared essays to one of the
titles given; others write completely irrelevant essays. Such
practices lower considerably the mark for the essay.

             WHAT MUSIC MEANS TO ME

     Music is a shadowy, indefinable thing. Many feel that
a group of notes fitted together with various other notes in
a certain sequence is not music at all, if it fails to please their
aristocratic ears; but I have often thought that any such
group, written for a purpose (however obscure said purpose
                            27

may now be) can be called music, and I persist in doing so,
to the chagrin of many.

      Popular music means a great deal to me. Since I have
little musical talent or taste, I could not sing it, nor yet
render it on an instrument, nor even judge which performer
is singing the tune in tune. It means, therefore, that I must
listen daily to the radio in order to ascertain what is “hot,”
“stale,” or “gone,” in order to appear well educated in the
matter to certain of my friends. Since my parents are not
always completely in sympathy with this burning desire for
knowledge, especially when they have a burning desire to
hear something else, popular music as sung on the radio
means strife. Popular music as sung by Me, however, is an
entirely different matter. There is no opposition here. It
means one of two things; either I must go upstairs and shut
my door, or I must wait until everyone is out, unless I wish
my rendition to be punctuated by rude laughter and coarse
jokes insinuating a relationship with the bull frog. Mention
must be made at this juncture of popular music as sung by
Someone Else and in many cases, such as “On Top of Old
Smokey” being rendered at this moment in a grating, jerky
undertone by the individual in front, (whose name must not
be disclosed) this performance means simply torture.

     Classical music, on the other hand, can mean two things.
I derive the utmost pleasure from hearing some beautiful
aria sung by Caruso, or his present-day usurper, whether
the record (in the case of Caruso) be scratched, chipped, or
cracked. It matters little whether I know what the words
mean, and I don’t; the important thing to me is the controlled
power of a voice which gives one the feeling that the per¬
former could lift off the roof, if he so chose. Classical music
performed by a symphony orchestra, however, means some¬
thing quite different. I close my eyes and, as the music
flows in one ear and, unfortunately, out the other, I picture
a vast desert, whipped by a scorching wind. I am delighted
because I feel the music. At this point the announcer usually
breaks in with a cheery, “You have just heard Beethoven’s
musical description of the Atlantic Ocean,” or something
equally disappointing.

     This treatise would be completely pointless, were it not
for the opportunity given me to sing Riligious music in a
church choir. This kind of music is, to me, different from
any other; I know it. Because I may read it, think about it,
and squeak it out quietly without fear of derision, I am
peculiarly attached to it. True, like the others, it means
                            28

superficial things like the removal of gum and candy before
choir practice, the necessity of keeping one eye on the music,
one on the organist, and the opportunity of giggling at such
phrases as “The bars of Heaven’s high portals.” But it also
means that although it is perfect and I am faulty, although
beautiful voices sing it every day, and I sing it with no voice
at all; yet I may take part in it just because I want to, and
this is the reason why I love it best of all.

                    FLYING SAUCERS

     The twentieth century has brought us many strange and

flying saucer. In this particular age, man’s scientific skill

The following essay was given a mark of 28, or 40% :


          PORTRAIT OF A GRANDMOTHER

      A short little old lady of seventy-nine, with silvery gray
hair and a slight bend when walking, my grandmother seems
the perfect symbol of the hardy English stock of many years
back. Behind the light blue eyes, you can see memories
and troubled years, which have passed but in so doing have
left their hurts and bruises. The wrinkles in her face and the
heavy hands show, that in her younger years she had to
work. But despite these features there is a placidness and a
look of hope, and love on her quaint old face.

     How annoying it is when you have to repeat everything
twice and explain things to her over and over again. But
when later thinking about this you do not really mind. And
I guess when she keeps telling you things such as “Wear
your rubbers,” “Eat you meals,” “Be home early,” they are
only for your own good. However, this incessant nagging
although meaning well, can become very monotonous and
as I said before annoying.

     The experienced advice she gives me is knowledge that
cannot be bought and is well worth listening to. This is a
universal truth as well, for we can see that the conservative
ideas of older people is good not only for the individual but
also for the nation. This is well illustrated by the English
House of Lords, where many retired peers, and lords are
kept in constant tact for their advice on matters of state.

    From these few example of older people who are famous,
and from the portrait of my grandmother we see that the
old people are necessary for the make up of our way of life.
And just as Mr. Shaw’s plays and Mr. Jolson’s records will
bring happiness to many people, my grandmother will bring
happiness to me.

IV. Question 4—The Prose Appreciation-—15 marks

1. General Comments

     (a) Students should paragraph their appreciations according
to the headings given.
     (b) Many students found difficulty in distinguishing be¬
tween diction and imagery. The marking scheme for the appreci¬
ation shows that no penalty was imposed for confusion of these
two terms. Diction properly refers to the choice and arrange¬
ment of words, imagery to the pictorial and sensuous effect
caused by figures of speech and by vivid description.

     (c) Some students gave as appreciations detailed gram¬
matical analysis of types of sentences. Others gave sentence-by¬
sentence commentaries. A few discussed merely the aptness of
punctuation in the passage.

     (d) Students are often careless of expression and sentence
structure in this type of question.      Style is important in the
writing of an appreciation.

     (e) Quotations from the appreciation should be worked into
sentences and set off by quotation marks. Few students have

    (f) In view of the total inability of a number of candidates
to write an appreciation, teachers are advised to give the precis

2. The Marking Scheme for the Appreciation
    Eleven marks were given for matter and 4 marks for style,
15 marks in all. Marks for matter were divided as follows:
paragraph and sentence effectiveness—6 marks; diction—2
marks; imagery—3 marks. Total—11 marks.

1. Paragraph Effectiveness—4 marks
    Any one of the following to rate four marks:
     (a) Analysis of any one or more paragraphs with regard to
unity, coherence, and emphasis; e.g., Paragraph one—
    Unity—all descriptive details refer to London and the effects
        of the raids.
    Coherence—details are given in a clear progressive order.
       The point of view of the narrator is maintained through¬
       out.
Full marks were given only if the candidate made reference to
more than one paragraph.
    Emphasis—arrangement of details to achieve climax in the
       last sentence.

    (b) Variety of paragraph purpose:.
        Paragraph 1—the setting, London during the air-raids.
        Paragraph 2—straight narration.
        Paragraph 3—argument.
        Paragraph 4—contrast.
If the candidate merely states the theme of each paragraph, no
more than 1 mark is to bevgiyen.
    (c) Variety of mood in the paragraphs.
         Paragraph 1—endurance.
         Paragraph 2—alarm.
         Paragraph 3—rational argument.
         Paragraph 4—emotional contrast or questioning.

(This approach was rarely attempted by candidates.)

2. Sentence Effectiveness—2 or 3 marks

     (a) The use of short sentences to give details in rapid suc¬
         cession to create tension, or for straight narration.

     (b) The use of longer sentences to give more complex im¬
         pression, or for contrast, or for emotional effect.


3. Diction—2 or 3 marks

     (a) The use of simple, ordinary, familiar diction.

     (b) The occasional use of a less familiar word; e.g., mag¬
         nanimity, contingent, acrid.

     (c) Effective use of adjectives; e.g., “tormenting” bombers,
         “frantic” writhings, the piling up of adjectives in the
         second last sentence, “accurate, ordered, disciplined.”


4. Imagery—2 or 3 marks

     (a) Clear word-pictures.

     (b) Use of metaphors and similes.

     (c) Use of imaginative personification; e.g. London.

     (d) Use of sharp contrast, as in the last paragraph.

     (e) Use of specific detail for realistic effect; e.g. place names,
         personal names, “Beethoven’s Archduke trio.”


NOTE: References to diction and imagery should be illustrated.

3. Marks for style

1.   Full marks should be given for a concise, coherent, competent
     style, free from errors.

2.   One mark should be deducted for the first major error, and
     one mark for each subsequent major error of a different type
     up to a maximum of three marks. Minor errors should also
     be taken into consideration in the final judgment.

3.   Repetition, irrelevance, and verbiage should be penalized.
     A point by point answer should be marked down for style.

4. Sample Appreciation

    The following appreciation was given 8 marks for matter
and 3 for style:

        The passage presents a vivid and powerful picture of
    London in the days of the Blitz. The author, in describing
    the terrible destruction wrought by the never-ending rain
    of bombs, looks upon the great city as a person suffering
    cruelty but in silence, offering no complaint against its lot.

         The passage is a well-connected one, with each sentence
    continuing the thought of the preceding one. The sentences
    themselves are not, on the whole, long ones, indeed, the
    opening sentence is very abrupt precipitating the reader
    directly into the content of the passage. The shorter sen¬
    tences deal more with facts, while the longer ones give the
    author scope for his vivid descriptive powers. The vocabu¬
    lary used has been carefully chosen and is comparatively
    easy to understand, although this by no means lessens the
    vividness of the passage. The paragraphs deal completely
    with one aspect of the general thought, with the lengthening
    of the first paragraph emphasizing its importance.

         Throughout the passage are found evidences of the
    author’s vivid phraseology. London is personified, and the
    first paragraph abounds with figures of speech. Ford heard
    “the laboured breath of London”—a colourful continuation
    of the personification of London. “The hammer stroke of a
    heavy bomb” is a powerful metaphor aptly describing the
    effect of the explosives. Imitative harmony appears in such
    words as “quivered” and “growling,” painting the picture
    even more vividly by describing the sounds and emotions
    of the city. The author describes the destruction of the
    bombs as “no more than cuts and sores upon the hide of some
    great, slow animal,” a vivid simile aptly picturing mammoth
    London’s reaction. The author’s vivid descriptive style is
    again apparent when he describes the “writhings” of girders
    and “acrid heaps” of rubble after a bomb has struck a build¬
    ing. These words imitate motion and smell respectively,
    and are well-suited to the passage.

         The author has shown a mastery of literary skill, and an
    ability to combine both the factual and the descriptive into
    an interesting well-composed passage.

V. Spelling

     (a) Students were penalized up to five marks for spelling
         errors.

     (b) Spelling errors this year averaged nearly two per paper.

     (c) Some of the more commonly misspelled words were:
aggression, alliteration, antecedent, appreciation, author, comma,
describe, description, exaggerate, excellent, except, explanation,

you’re.

VI. Legibility

    Teachers should advise students to adopt a readable script
and to make greater use of the blank pages of the examination
booklet for rough work. Care in arrangement and legibility are
decided assets to a candidate in Upper School English
Composition.
